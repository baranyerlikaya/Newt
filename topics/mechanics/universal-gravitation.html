Universal Law of Gravitation

Newton's inverse-square law of gravity F = GMm/r², the universal gravitational constant G, and how the familiar mg near Earth's surface comes out of it.

In short

Every pair of masses pulls on each other, along the line joining them. The strength is 1; 2:

$$F = \frac{GMm}{r^2},$$

where $G \approx 6.67\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ is a universal constant and $r$ is the distance between the centres.

The inverse square is key: double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. That's why gravity is strong at Earth's surface but essentially irrelevant between people in the same room.

Near Earth's surface, $r$ doesn't change much as you move around, so the formula reduces to $F = mg$ with 2:

$$g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2} \approx 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

On the Moon, $g \approx 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2$ (about $1/6$ Earth's) because the Moon is much less massive even though it's smaller. Astronauts don't lose weight by being far from Earth; their weight is actually just the Moon's gravity pulling them 1.

Weight is a force; mass is not. Your mass is the same everywhere. Your weight depends on the local $g$ 2; 3.

Everything falls the same. Drop a hammer and a feather on the Moon (no air): they land together. This happens because the $m$ in $F = GMm/r^2$ is the same $m$ as in $F = ma$, so acceleration $a = GM/r^2$ doesn't depend on $m$ 2; 3.

Theory

Newton's law of universal gravitation

Two point masses $M$ and $m$ a distance $r$ apart attract each other along the line joining them, with force 1 §9.2.1; 2 §8.2.1:

$$\mathbf F = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat r,$$

where $\hat r$ points from the source $M$ to the body $m$, and the minus sign makes the force attractive. $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant 1:

$$G = 6.674\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2.$$

The law is universal: the same $G$ applies to every pair of masses — planets, apples and galaxies alike 1 §9.2.

Spherical bodies

A beautiful consequence (which Newton proved using calculus): a spherically symmetric body attracts an external particle as if all its mass were concentrated at its centre 2 §8.2.3. So for an object of mass $m$ at distance $r$ from the centre of a planet of mass $M$ and radius $R_p$ ($r \ge R_p$):

$$F = \frac{GMm}{r^2}.$$

Gravity near Earth's surface

At the Earth's surface ($r = R_E$) the gravitational acceleration is 2 §8.2.3:

$$g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2} \approx 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

With $M_E = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ and $R_E = 6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$, this works out exactly. The familiar $F = mg$ is just the inverse-square law evaluated at $r = R_E$ for everyday heights where $r \approx R_E$ stays constant to high accuracy 2.

Mass and weight

The weight of a body is the gravitational force on it; at Earth's surface, $W = mg$. This is a force in newtons 2 §8.2.3. The body's mass $m$ is the same everywhere — what changes with location (Earth, Moon, orbit) is $g$, and therefore the weight.

Inertial = gravitational mass

The $m$ in $F = GMm/r^2$ (gravitational mass) is experimentally identical to the $m$ in $F = ma$ (inertial mass). That is why every body falls with the same acceleration $g$ near the surface, regardless of mass. In Einstein's general relativity this equality becomes the equivalence principle 2 §8.2.2; 3 §Measurement of Mass.

Note

Gravity between everyday objects is tiny. Two $1\ \text{kg}$ masses $1\ \text{m}$ apart attract each other with $F \approx 6.7\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}$ — undetectable without a torsion balance. Gravity dominates only when one of the masses is astronomical 1.

Warning

The $r$ in $F = GMm/r^2$ is the distance between centres (for spherical bodies), not the height above a surface. For a particle at height $h$ above Earth's surface, $r = R_E + h$ 2 §8.2.3.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Verify that Newton's law gives $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ at Earth's surface. Use $M_E = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$, $R_E = 6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$. (From 2, §8.2.3.)

  1. $g = GM_E/R_E^2 = (6.674\times 10^{-11})(5.97\times 10^{24})/(6.37\times 10^6)^2$.

  2. Numerator: $3.98\times 10^{14}$. Denominator: $4.06\times 10^{13}$.

  3. $g \approx 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ ✓.

Check

Satellite in low Earth orbit ($h \sim 400\ \text{km}$): $r = R_E + h \approx 6.77\times 10^6\ \text{m}$, so $g$ there is $9.81\cdot(R_E/r)^2 \approx 8.7\ \text{m/s}^2$ — only slightly reduced. Astronauts float not because gravity is weak, but because they are in free fall.

Example

Earth's Moon ($M_M = 7.35\times 10^{22}\ \text{kg}$, $R_M = 1.74\times 10^6\ \text{m}$). Find its surface gravity. (Standard application of $g = GM/R^2$.)

  1. $g_M = GM_M/R_M^2 = (6.674\times 10^{-11})(7.35\times 10^{22})/(1.74\times 10^6)^2$.

  2. $\approx 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2 \approx g/6$.

Check

Apollo astronauts weighed $1/6$ their Earth weight and bounced accordingly ✓.

Example

What is the acceleration of the Moon toward Earth, and how does it compare with the centripetal acceleration of its orbit? (From 1, §9.2 — Newton's classic moon test.)

  1. Moon–Earth distance $r = 3.84\times 10^8\ \text{m}$, $M_E = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$.

  2. $a_{\text{grav}} = GM_E/r^2 \approx 2.70\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  3. Centripetal for circular orbit: $a_c = \omega^2 r = (2\pi/T)^2 r$, $T = 27.3\ \text{days}$: $a_c \approx 2.72\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Check

The two match: gravity is exactly the centripetal force of the Moon's orbit. Newton's famous proof that terrestrial gravity and planetary motion come from the same law ✓.
