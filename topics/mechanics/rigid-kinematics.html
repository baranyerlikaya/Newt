Rigid Body Kinematics

A rigid body's motion decomposes into translation of the centre of mass and rotation about an axis through the CM. Each point of the body has its own velocity v = V_CM + ω × r'.

In short

A rigid body is one that doesn't deform. All its particles stay at fixed distances from each other. So its entire motion is captured by 1; 2:

  • Where the centre of mass is going (translation).
  • How it's spinning about the CM (rotation).

Every single point's velocity is 1:

$$\mathbf v = \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'.$$

$\mathbf V_{\text{CM}}$ is the same for the whole body; $\mathbf r'$ is each point's offset from the CM; $\boldsymbol\omega$ is the angular velocity (same for the whole body).

Rolling without slipping (Topic 11.3): the contact point is instantaneously at rest. That means $V_{\text{CM}} = \omega R$. The top of the wheel moves at twice the bike's forward speed.

For spinning about a fixed axis, rotational kinematics copies translational 2:

  • $v = r_\perp\omega$ (speed of a point)
  • $a_t = r_\perp\alpha$ (tangential acceleration)
  • $a_c = r_\perp\omega^2$ (centripetal)
  • Constant $\alpha$: $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$.

Same formulas as straight-line motion, with $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$.

Theory

What makes a body rigid

A rigid body is a system of particles with fixed distances between every pair. Internal forces just enforce the constraints — they do no net work. A rigid body in 3D has six degrees of freedom: three for the position of its CM, three for its orientation 1 §20.2; 2 §16.2.

Chasles' theorem: translation + rotation

The most general motion of a rigid body at any instant is a translation of the CM plus a rotation about an axis through the CM 1 §20.2. Each particle's velocity is:

$$\mathbf v_i = \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i',$$

where $\mathbf r_i'$ is the particle's position relative to the CM and $\boldsymbol\omega$ is the (instantaneous) angular velocity, the same for every particle of the body.

Fixed-axis rotation

A special and common case: the body rotates about a fixed axis (axle of a wheel, hinge of a door). Choose the axis as $\hat z$. Then $\boldsymbol\omega = \omega\hat z$, every point moves in a circle in a plane perpendicular to the axis, with speed $v = r_\perp\omega$ 2 §16.2:

  • Angular velocity $\omega = d\theta/dt$.
  • Angular acceleration $\alpha = d\omega/dt = d^2\theta/dt^2$.
  • Tangential acceleration $a_t = r_\perp\alpha$.
  • Centripetal acceleration $a_c = r_\perp\omega^2$.

General 3D rotations

For rotations about a general axis through the CM, $\boldsymbol\omega$ is a vector along that axis. For free rotation (no torques), $\boldsymbol\omega$ can tumble over time — Euler angles describe the full orientation. In an introductory course we usually restrict to fixed-axis rotations or to symmetry axes where $\boldsymbol\omega$ stays put 1 §20.2, §20.3.

Kinematic equations for rotations

For constant $\alpha$, the rotational formulas mirror the translational ones exactly 2 §16.2:

$$\omega(t) = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2.$$

And the handy "square" identity:

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0).$$

Note

Every point of a rigid body has the same $\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\boldsymbol\alpha$ (this is what "rigid" means — otherwise the body would be deforming) 2 §16.2.

Warning

Linear velocities $\mathbf v_i$ differ from point to point (they are $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i'$). Only $\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\boldsymbol\alpha$ are "properties of the whole body" 2.

Visualization

A wheel rolls without slipping to the right. The CM moves at steady speed $v$; each rim point does a cycloid. Watch the small dot marking a specific rim point: it is at rest whenever it touches the ground, moves at $2v$ when at the top, and in between moves in curved paths. This is $\mathbf v = \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'$ in action.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping with CM velocity $V$. Find the velocity of the top, bottom and side points of the rim. (From 1, §20.2.)

  1. Rolling without slipping means $V = \omega R$, with $\boldsymbol\omega$ along the axle (into the page, say, for rightward motion).

  2. Top: $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'$ where $\mathbf r' = +R\hat y$ gives $\omega R\hat x = +V\hat x$. Adding $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}} = V\hat x$: top moves at $2V$ forward.

  3. Bottom: $\mathbf r' = -R\hat y$ gives $-V\hat x$. Adding $V\hat x$: bottom is instantaneously at rest.

  4. Side points: $\mathbf r' = \pm R\hat x$, gives $\mp V\hat y$ (upward or downward). Adding $V\hat x$: moves diagonally at $\sqrt 2\,V$.

Check

Top of a bike wheel moves twice as fast as the bike; bottom is at rest (which is why rolling doesn't slip); sides trace a cycloid, as in Topic 2.6 ✓.

Example

A record turntable starts from rest and reaches $33\tfrac13\ \text{rpm}$ in $2\ \text{s}$. What is $\alpha$, and how many revolutions does it make? (Standard kinematics.)

  1. $\omega_f = 33.33\times 2\pi/60 \approx 3.49\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. $\alpha = \omega_f/t = 3.49/2 \approx 1.75\ \text{rad/s}^2$.

  3. $\Delta\theta = \tfrac12\alpha t^2 = \tfrac12\times 1.75\times 4 = 3.49\ \text{rad} \approx 0.56$ revolutions.

Check

Average $\omega$ during spin-up is $\omega_f/2$, so $\Delta\theta = (\omega_f/2)t = 3.49\ \text{rad}$ ✓.

Example

A ball of radius $R$ rolls without slipping down a slope and has angular speed $\omega = 10\ \text{rad/s}$ when $R = 0.05\ \text{m}$. Find its linear speed, period, tangential and centripetal accelerations at the rim. (Direct application.)

  1. $v_{\text{CM}} = R\omega = 0.5\ \text{m/s}$.

  2. Period of one full rotation: $T = 2\pi/\omega \approx 0.63\ \text{s}$.

  3. If $\alpha = 0$ (constant $\omega$), $a_t = 0$; centripetal of a rim point about the axle: $a_c = R\omega^2 = 0.05\times 100 = 5\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Check

Centripetal is from the point being in circular motion about the CM. In the ground frame, a different calculation applies ✓.
