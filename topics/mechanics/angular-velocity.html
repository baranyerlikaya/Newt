Angular Velocity

The angular velocity ω = dθ/dt: how fast an angle changes with time, its direction along the axis of rotation, and the link v = ωR between angular and linear speed.

In short

How fast does something spin? If you turn by angle $\theta$ in time $t$, your angular velocity is 1; 2

$$\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}.$$

Measured in radians per second. A record player at 33⅓ rpm turns at about 3.5 rad/s.

Direction? Along the axis you're spinning around, not along the motion. Curl the fingers of your right hand the way the thing is spinning; your thumb shows where $\boldsymbol\omega$ points 2.

Speed of a point on the circle. The further from the axis, the faster. For a point at distance $R$ from the axis 1; 2:

$$v = R\omega.$$

That's why the end of a cracked whip moves so fast, and why skaters tuck in to spin faster.

Still accelerating: even at a steady $\omega$ (uniform circular motion), the point's direction changes, so there's an acceleration toward the centre: $a_c = \omega^2 R = v^2/R$ 2.

Speeding the spin up: if $\omega$ itself changes, the angular acceleration $\alpha = d\omega/dt$ is the rotational version of $a$ 2. With constant $\alpha$, the formulas are exactly the linear ones with $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$.

Period: one full turn takes $T = 2\pi/\omega$ seconds 1.

Theory

Angle, angular velocity, angular acceleration

For a particle on a circle of radius $R$, the position can be given by the angle $\theta(t)$ between the radial line and the $+x$-axis 1 §Circular Motion; 2 §16.2.2. The angular velocity and angular acceleration are its time derivatives:

$$\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.$$

Units: $\omega$ in rad/s, $\alpha$ in rad/s². The sign of $\omega$ tells the sense: $\omega > 0$ for counterclockwise motion, $\omega < 0$ for clockwise 2 §16.2.3.

Angular velocity as a vector

For rotation about a fixed axis, $\boldsymbol\omega$ is a vector along that axis, with its direction fixed by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers in the sense of rotation, the thumb gives $\boldsymbol\omega$ 2 §16.2.2. Taking the axis along $\hat k$,

$$\boldsymbol\omega = \omega\,\hat k.$$

All points of a rigid body share the same $\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\boldsymbol\alpha$, otherwise they would drift relative to each other 2 §16.2.2.

Linear from angular: v = ωR

A point at distance $R$ from the axis traces a circle. Its arc length over time $dt$ is $ds = R\,d\theta$, so its tangential speed is 1 §Circular Motion; 2 §16.2.4:

$$v = R\omega.$$

If $\omega$ itself changes, the point has a tangential acceleration along its motion and a centripetal one toward the centre 2 §16.2.4:

$$a_t = R\alpha, \qquad a_c = R\omega^2 = \frac{v^2}{R}.$$

They are perpendicular, so the total acceleration has magnitude $\sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2}$.

Period and frequency

If $\omega$ is constant (uniform rotation), the angle grows linearly, $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega t$, and one full turn takes the period 1 §Circular Motion:

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}.$$

$f$ is the frequency in hertz (1 Hz = 1 cycle per second). "Angular frequency" $\omega$ is the rate of change of the phase, measured in rad/s 1.

Constant angular acceleration

When $\alpha$ is constant, the formulas are just the linear ones with symbols replaced 2 §16.2.2:

$$\omega(t) = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2.$$

Note

In the formula $v = R\omega$, the angle must be in radians. Degrees would introduce an extra factor $\pi/180$. All rotational formulas on this page assume radians 1 §Circular Motion.

Warning

$\omega$ and $\alpha$ point along the axis of rotation, not along the velocity of the moving point. For a wheel spinning in the plane of the page, $\boldsymbol\omega$ points out of (or into) the page 2 §16.2.2.

Visualization

A dot moves around a circle at angular velocity $\omega$. The velocity arrow (tangent to the circle) has length $\omega R$ — speed up the slider and the dot flies around faster, and the arrow gets longer. The up-pointing arrow shows $\boldsymbol\omega$ pointing out of the page by the right-hand rule, growing with $\omega$. Add small $\alpha$: the speed grows (or shrinks) over time. The readouts show $\omega$, $\theta$, and the period $T = 2\pi/\omega$.

Worked examples

Example

A record on a turntable spins at a steady $33\tfrac13$ revolutions per minute. Find its angular velocity in rad/s, its period, and the tangential speed of a point at $R = 10\ \text{cm}$. (From 2, Example 16.1, in SI units.)

  1. $f = 33.33/60 \approx 0.556\ \text{Hz}$, so $\omega = 2\pi f \approx 3.49\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. Period: $T = 1/f \approx 1.80\ \text{s}$.

  3. Tangential speed: $v = R\omega = 0.10 \times 3.49 \approx 0.35\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

Distance per revolution at $R = 10\ \text{cm}$: $2\pi R \approx 0.628\ \text{m}$, covered in $T = 1.80\ \text{s}$: $v = 0.628/1.80 \approx 0.35\ \text{m/s}$. ✓

Example

A particle moves on a circle of radius $R$ with $\theta(t) = \omega t$ (constant $\omega$). Find the position, velocity and acceleration. (From 1, Worked Example “Uniform Circular Motion”.)

  1. Position: $\mathbf r = R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath$.

  2. Velocity: $\mathbf v = -R\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + R\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath$, with magnitude $v = R\omega$.

  3. Acceleration: $\mathbf a = -R\omega^2\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\imath - R\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath = -\omega^2\,\mathbf r$: toward the centre, magnitude $R\omega^2$.

Check

$\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v = 0$: velocity is tangent to the circle ✓. And $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v = 0$ for uniform motion ✓.

Example

A wheel starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $\alpha = 2\ \text{rad/s}^2$. How many revolutions does it make in the first $5\ \text{s}$, and what is its angular velocity then? (Standard constant-$\alpha$ application, 2 §16.2.2.)

  1. $\omega(5) = \omega_0 + \alpha t = 0 + 2 \times 5 = 10\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. $\theta(5) = \tfrac12 \alpha t^2 = \tfrac12 \times 2 \times 25 = 25\ \text{rad}$.

  3. Revolutions: $25/(2\pi) \approx 3.98$.

Check

Average $\omega$ over the interval is $5\ \text{rad/s}$, so $\theta = 5 \times 5 = 25\ \text{rad}$ ✓.
