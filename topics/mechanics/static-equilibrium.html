Static Equilibrium

A rigid body in static equilibrium has zero net force AND zero net torque. The two conditions let you solve for the forces on bridges, ladders, seesaws and bookshelves.

In short

Static equilibrium: an object stays put. Not just no sliding — also no spinning. So two conditions have to hold at once 1:

$$\sum\mathbf F = 0, \qquad \sum\boldsymbol\tau = 0.$$
  • Forces balance in every direction.
  • Torques balance about every point.

The clever-pivot trick: you can take torques about any pivot. Pick one that goes through an unknown force so its torque vanishes — now you don't need to solve for it 1.

Lever law (Archimedes): balance is about torques, not forces.

$$F_L\,d_L = F_R\,d_R.$$

Small force far from the pivot = large force near it. Why crowbars and wrenches work — and why a 30 kg kid can balance a 20 kg one on a seesaw by sitting closer in.

Centre of gravity: gravity acts as if it were applied at the CM 2. A building, a leaning tower, a Jenga tower stays up as long as the vertical line through its CG stays inside its base. Walk that line past the edge and it topples.

Three-step recipe: free-body diagram → $\sum F = 0$ for each axis → $\sum\tau = 0$ about a smart pivot.

Theory

Two conditions

A rigid body is in static equilibrium if every point of it is at rest in an inertial frame. Then both $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}}$ and $\boldsymbol\omega$ are zero, and their derivatives also vanish, so 1 §18.1:

$$\sum_i\mathbf F_i = 0, \qquad \sum_i\boldsymbol\tau_i = 0.$$

The first says the body as a whole doesn't accelerate linearly; the second says it doesn't spin up. Both are vector equations — three components each in 3D, two + one in 2D.

Choice of pivot

For a body in equilibrium, the torque equation $\sum\boldsymbol\tau = 0$ holds about any point. Pick the point that kills the most unknowns: choose the pivot through an unknown force so its torque vanishes 1 §18.2. Common tricks:

  • Ladder against a wall: take the pivot at the foot, and the ground's forces drop out.
  • Seesaw: take the pivot at the fulcrum, and its reaction drops out.

Lever law

For a horizontal beam pivoted somewhere, in equilibrium 1 §18.2:

$$F_L\,d_L = F_R\,d_R,$$

where $F$'s are perpendicular forces applied on each side and $d$'s are the moment arms from the pivot. This is Archimedes' lever law — apply a small force at a long moment arm to balance a large force at a short one.

In general ("generalized lever law") 1 §18.3, the torque is the force times the perpendicular distance, or equivalently the perpendicular component of the force times the distance.

Centre of gravity

In uniform gravity, each part of a body has weight $m_i g$ downward, so the total gravitational force on the body is $Mg$ acting as if applied at the centre of mass — called the centre of gravity (CG) 2 §17.4.2. For non-uniform gravity, CM and CG differ slightly.

Consequence: for a body on a flat surface, it topples when the vertical line through its CG falls outside its base of support. Leaning towers, Jenga blocks, and pensioners carrying heavy bags all obey this 2 §17.4.2.

Three-step recipe for statics problems

1 §18.4:

  1. Identify the body and draw a free-body diagram with every external force (gravity at CG, normal forces, friction, tensions).
  2. Write $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$ (and $\sum F_z = 0$ if needed).
  3. Write $\sum\tau = 0$ about a clever pivot.

In 2D you get three equations, enough to solve for up to three unknown components.

Note

"Equilibrium" here means static: motionless. A body moving at constant velocity also has $\sum\mathbf F = 0$ and $\sum\boldsymbol\tau = 0$ — "dynamic equilibrium". Static just adds the extra condition of zero velocity 1.

Warning

Torque depends on the chosen pivot. $\sum\boldsymbol\tau = 0$ holds at every pivot only if $\sum\mathbf F = 0$ also holds — these two conditions are coupled 1 §18.2.

Visualization

A rod with a force applied at its end. For static equilibrium about a chosen pivot, the torque $\tau = rF\sin\phi$ must be zero (or balanced by other torques). Vary the angle $\phi$ to see how torque changes: maximum when the force is perpendicular ($\phi = 90°$), zero when parallel. In a real statics problem you add up all the torques and set the sum to zero.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A $20\ \text{kg}$ child sits $2\ \text{m}$ from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a $30\ \text{kg}$ child sit to balance it? (From 1, §18.2.)

  1. Lever law about the pivot: $20g\times 2 = 30g\times d$.

  2. $d = 40/30 = 4/3 \approx 1.33\ \text{m}$ on the other side.

Check

Heavier child sits closer to the pivot — ratio of distances is the inverse of the mass ratio ✓.

Example

A uniform ladder of mass $M$ and length $L$ leans against a frictionless wall at angle $\theta$ to the vertical. Friction at the floor has coefficient $\mu$. What is the minimum $\mu$ for the ladder not to slip? (Standard statics; from 1, §18.4.)

  1. Forces: $Mg$ at CG (middle of ladder), normal from wall $N_w$ (horizontal), normal from floor $N_f$ (vertical), friction $f$ (horizontal).

  2. $\sum F_y = 0$: $N_f = Mg$. $\sum F_x = 0$: $f = N_w$.

  3. Take pivot at the foot: $\sum\tau = 0$ gives $Mg(L/2)\sin\theta = N_w L\cos\theta$, so $N_w = \tfrac12 Mg\tan\theta$.

  4. No-slip: $f \le \mu N_f$, i.e. $\tfrac12 Mg\tan\theta \le \mu Mg$, so $\mu \ge \tfrac12\tan\theta$.

Check

At $\theta = 45°$: $\mu \ge 0.5$. Steep ladders (small $\theta$ from vertical) are safe with less friction; shallow leans need more friction ✓.

Example

A bookshelf of width $w$ and height $h$ sits on the floor. If you push horizontally at the top with force $F$, how big must $F$ be (relative to the weight $W$) to start the shelf tipping? (Standard "topple" calculation.)

  1. Tipping occurs about the far edge. At that instant the normal force concentrates at the edge and the shelf is on the verge of rotating.

  2. $\sum\tau$ about the tipping edge: $Fh = W(w/2)$.

  3. $F_{\text{tip}} = Ww/(2h)$. Wide shelf or short shelf: hard to tip. Tall narrow shelf: easy.

Check

Why a bookshelf is bolted to the wall: $h/w$ is big and even modest horizontal forces can tip it ✓.
