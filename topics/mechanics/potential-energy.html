Conservative Forces and Potential Energy

A conservative force is one whose work depends only on the endpoints, never on the path. Then there is a potential energy U(r) with F = −∇U, and the mechanical energy K + U is conserved.

In short

A conservative force is one where only the start and end matter. Lift a book by a path that goes up, sideways and down — gravity doesn't care how; it only looks at the height difference 1; 2.

Examples that are conservative: gravity, springs, electric forces. Examples that aren't: friction, air drag — rubbing in circles clearly costs energy even though you end where you started.

For a conservative force we can define potential energy $U(\mathbf r)$, so that 1; 2:

$$W_{AB} = U_A - U_B.$$

Big $U$ means "if I let go, I'll move toward smaller $U$". The force is the downhill slope of $U$:

$$F = -\frac{dU}{dx} \quad (\text{1D}), \qquad \mathbf F = -\nabla U \quad (\text{3D}).$$

Three to memorize:

  • A thing at height $y$ above the ground: $U = mgy$ 2.
  • A spring stretched by $x$: $U = \tfrac12 k x^2$ 2.
  • Gravity far from Earth, distance $r$ from the centre: $U = -GMm/r$ 2.

The zero is up to you. Only differences matter — so pick the zero wherever it's convenient (floor for gravity, natural length for a spring, infinity for far-field gravity) 1.

Equilibrium points are flat spots of U: a bottom (local minimum) is stable (pushes you back), a top (local maximum) is unstable (shoves you away) 1.

Theory

Conservative forces

A force is conservative if the work it does in moving a particle between two points is independent of the path taken 1 §Conservative Forces and Potential Energy; 2 §14.2. Equivalently, the work around any closed loop is zero:

$$\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = 0.$$

Examples: gravity (near and far), the Hooke's-law spring force, the electrostatic force. Non-conservative: friction, drag, muscles — the work they do depends on the path (think of rubbing in a circle versus not rubbing at all) 2 §14.2.

Definition of potential energy

For a conservative force, define the potential energy $U(\mathbf r)$ so that the work equals the drop in $U$ 1; 2 §14.3:

$$W_{AB} = \int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = U(\mathbf r_A) - U(\mathbf r_B) = -\Delta U.$$

$U$ is a scalar function of position; adding a constant doesn't change the force, so the zero of $U$ is arbitrary — a convention 1.

Force from potential energy

Differentiating the definition gives the force 1; 2 §14.3:

$$F_x = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \qquad \mathbf F = -\nabla U.$$

In 1D: $F(x) = -dU/dx$. The force points "downhill" on the potential energy graph: toward lower $U$. Maxima of $U$ repel the particle; minima attract it 1 §More on the Potential Energy.

Standard potential energies

Three that recur constantly 2 §14.4:

  • Uniform gravity near Earth, height $y$: $U = mgy$, so $F = -mg\,\hat\jmath$ 2 §14.4.1.
  • Hooke's-law spring, displacement $x$ from the natural length: $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$, so $F = -kx$ 2 §14.4.2.
  • Newtonian gravity (point masses): $U = -GMm/r$, so $F_r = -GMm/r^2$ (attractive, inverse-square) 2 §14.4.3.

Finding U from F and the arbitrary constant

For a 1D conservative force $F(x)$, integrate to get the potential energy 1; 2 §14.4:

$$U(x) = -\int F(x)\,dx + C.$$

The integration constant $C$ is fixed by choosing a reference where $U = 0$. For uniform gravity people usually pick the ground; for Newtonian gravity, infinity ($U(\infty) = 0$, which forces $U = -GMm/r$); for springs, the natural length 2 §14.4.

Note

Every one-dimensional force that depends only on position is automatically conservative (because its work $\int F\,dx$ depends only on the endpoints). In two or more dimensions the conservative condition is a real restriction 2 §14.2.

Warning

Only the change in $U$ has physical meaning; $U(\mathbf r)$ itself depends on an arbitrary reference. Choose the zero to make the problem simple, but keep the choice consistent throughout a calculation 1.

Visualization

A particle moves in one dimension under the potential $U(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$, a "double well". The dark dot sits on the curve at the particle's current $x$, its height $U(x)$. The dashed line shows the total energy $E$; the vertical segment above the particle is the kinetic energy $K = E - U$. Set $E$ small: the particle rattles in one well ($x \approx -1$ or $+1$, local minima = stable equilibria). Set $E > 0$: it crosses the central hump at $x = 0$ (local max, unstable equilibrium) and visits both wells.

Worked examples

Example

Show that the uniform gravitational force near the Earth is conservative, and find its potential energy $U(y)$. (From 1, §More on the Potential Energy.)

  1. Force: $\mathbf F = -mg\,\hat\jmath$. Work from $(x_A, y_A)$ to $(x_B, y_B)$ along any path: $W = \int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = -mg\int dy = -mg(y_B - y_A)$.

  2. Depends only on $y_A, y_B$, not on the path — the force is conservative.

  3. Define $U(y) = mgy + C$. Check: $-dU/dy = -mg = F_y$ ✓. Choosing $U(0) = 0$ gives $U = mgy$.

Check

Closed loop: start and end at same $y$, so $W = 0$ ✓.

Example

For the spring force $F = -kx$, find $U(x)$ with $U(0) = 0$. Verify $F = -dU/dx$. (From 2, §14.4.2.)

  1. $U(x) = -\int_0^x F(x')\,dx' = -\int_0^x(-kx')\,dx' = \tfrac12 kx^2$.

  2. Check: $-dU/dx = -kx = F$ ✓.

Check

$U(x) \ge 0$ with minimum at $x = 0$, where the force vanishes — a stable equilibrium.

Example

Show that Newtonian gravity $\mathbf F = -(GMm/r^2)\hat r$ is conservative and find its potential energy with $U(\infty) = 0$. (From 2, §14.4.3.)

  1. In spherical coordinates the force has only a radial component $F_r = -GMm/r^2$, so $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = F_r\,dr$ depends only on $r$: conservative.

  2. $U(r) = -\int_\infty^r F_r\,dr' = -\int_\infty^r\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r'^2}\right)dr' = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}$.

  3. Check: $-dU/dr = -GMm/r^2 = F_r$ ✓. $U(\infty) = 0$ ✓.

Check

$U < 0$ everywhere: bringing masses from infinity releases energy, so bound states have negative total energy. ✓

Example

A particle moves in one dimension with $U(x) = k(x^3 - 3x)$ where $k > 0$. Find the equilibrium points and classify them. (From 1, §More on the Potential Energy.)

  1. $F = -dU/dx = -k(3x^2 - 3) = -3k(x^2 - 1)$. Equilibria at $F = 0$: $x = \pm1$.

  2. $U''(x) = 6kx$. At $x = 1$: $U''(1) = 6k > 0$: $U$ has a local minimum, so stable equilibrium.

  3. At $x = -1$: $U''(-1) = -6k < 0$: local maximum, so unstable equilibrium.

Check

Compute $U$ directly: $U(1) = k(1 - 3) = -2k$ (bottom of the well), $U(-1) = k(-1 + 3) = 2k$ (top of the barrier) ✓.
