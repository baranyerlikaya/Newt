Work and Kinetic Energy, Work–Energy Theorem

Work is force times displacement, kinetic energy is ½mv²; the work-energy theorem says that the total work done on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy.

In short

Kinetic energy is the "motion energy" of something moving. Twice the mass → twice the energy; twice the speed → four times the energy [1]; [2]:

$$K = \tfrac12 m v^2.$$

Measured in joules (J). A $1\ \text{kg}$ book at $1\ \text{m/s}$ has $0.5\ \text{J}$; a car at $15\ \text{m/s}$ has about $170\ \text{kJ}$.

Work = force times how far it moves the thing, in the direction of the force [1]; [2]:

$$W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d.$$

Push a box 5 m with a 10 N force along the floor: $W = 50\ \text{J}$. Push sideways on a car moving down the road: $W = 0$, because your push isn't along the motion.

The work-energy theorem ties it all together [1]; [2]:

$$\boxed{W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K.}$$

Doing positive work on something speeds it up; doing negative work (like brakes) slows it down.

Power is how fast you do work [1]:

$$P = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.$$

Measured in watts. A person climbing stairs at a good pace puts out about 100 W; a fit cyclist peaks above 1 kW; a car engine delivers tens to hundreds of kW.

A perpendicular force does no work — the string on a swinging ball, the normal force on a sliding block, gravity on a horizontal walk [2].

Theory

Kinetic energy

The kinetic energy of a particle of mass $m$ moving with speed $v$ is [1, §Work and Kinetic Energy]; [2, §13.2]:

$$K = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v.$$

It is a scalar, never negative, and in SI has units of joules (J): $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2 = 1\ \text{N·m}$.

Work done by a force

When a force $\mathbf F$ acts on a particle that moves along an infinitesimal displacement $d\mathbf r$, the work done is [1]; [2, §13.4]:

$$dW = \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = F\,|d\mathbf r|\,\cos\phi,$$

where $\phi$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. For a path from $\mathbf r_1$ to $\mathbf r_2$ [1]:

$$W_{12} = \int_{\mathbf r_1}^{\mathbf r_2}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.$$

A force perpendicular to the motion does no work (centripetal force, normal force on a sliding block). A force with a component along the motion does positive work; a force opposing the motion does negative work [2, §13.4].

For a constant force and a straight displacement, $W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = Fd\cos\phi$ [2, §13.4].

The work-energy theorem

Along the path, Newton's second law gives $\mathbf F = m\dot{\mathbf v}$. Then [1, §Work and Kinetic Energy]; [2, §13.6]:

$$\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = m\dot{\mathbf v}\cdot\mathbf v\,dt = \tfrac12 m\,d(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v) = dK.$$

Integrating:

$$\boxed{W_{\text{net}} = K_2 - K_1 = \Delta K,}$$

the work-energy theorem: the total work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy [1]; [2, §13.6]. Positive net work speeds the particle up; negative net work slows it down.

Common calculations

Three cases worth having memorized [2, §13.4]:

  • Constant force along the motion: $W = Fd$. If the force opposes the motion, $W = -Fd$.
  • Gravity near the surface, straight-up displacement $h$: $W_{\text{grav}} = -mgh$ (falling body: $+mgh$).
  • Spring force $F = -kx$ stretched from 0 to $x_0$: $W_{\text{spring}} = -\tfrac12 kx_0^2$ (negative, since the spring opposes the stretch). The agent who stretches does $+\tfrac12 kx_0^2$.

Power

The rate at which a force does work is the power [1, §Impulse and Power]:

$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.$$

Positive when the force and velocity are in the same direction; negative when they oppose. Total work over an interval: $W = \int P\,dt$ [1].

Note

Only the net work matters for $\Delta K$, but each force does its own work individually. In a car braking, friction does negative work on the car, converting kinetic energy into heat [2, §13.6].

Warning

Work depends on the path in general. For a force perpendicular to the motion (normal force on a sliding block, centripetal force in a circle), $W = 0$ even if the body moves a long way [2, §13.4].

Visualization

A mass on a spring, released from stretch $x = 1$. The spring force $F = -kx$ does positive work when it points along the motion (pulling the mass toward the centre) and negative work when it opposes (slowing it past the centre). The oscillation shows $K$ turning into spring potential energy and back — that is the next page. For now: watch $x(t)$ (the lower graph) and notice that the maximum speed happens at $x = 0$, where $K$ is largest.

Worked examples

Example

A force $\mathbf F = (3\hat\imath + 4\hat\jmath)\ \text{N}$ moves an object through displacement $\mathbf d = (2\hat\imath - \hat\jmath)\ \text{m}$. Find the work. (From the course's dot-product example, [4, Example 3(d)].)

  1. $W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = (3)(2) + (4)(-1) = 6 - 4 = 2\ \text{J}$.

  2. Only the component of $\mathbf F$ along $\mathbf d$ does work.

Check

$|\mathbf F| = 5$, $|\mathbf d| = \sqrt5$, so $\cos\phi = 2/(5\sqrt5) \approx 0.179$ and $Fd\cos\phi = 5\sqrt5\times 0.179 \approx 2$ ✓.

Example

A spring of force constant $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$ is stretched from $0$ to $x_0 = 0.1\ \text{m}$. How much work does the person stretching do? (From [4], Worked Example 7(d).)

  1. The applied force equals the spring force in magnitude but opposite sign: $F_{\text{applied}}(x) = +kx$.

  2. $W = \int_0^{x_0}kx\,dx = \tfrac12 k x_0^2 = \tfrac12 \times 200 \times 0.01 = 1\ \text{J}$.

Check

The spring stores exactly this as potential energy (see Topic 5.3). ✓

Example

A $1500\ \text{kg}$ car at $15\ \text{m/s}$ brakes to rest in $20\ \text{m}$. Find the average braking force. (Application of the work-energy theorem, [1]; [2, §13.6].)

  1. Change in kinetic energy: $\Delta K = 0 - \tfrac12(1500)(15^2) = -1.69\times10^5\ \text{J}$.

  2. Work by friction: $W = -F d = -F \times 20$ (force opposes motion).

  3. Work-energy theorem: $-20F = -1.69\times10^5$, so $F \approx 8440\ \text{N}$.

Check

Deceleration: $a = F/m = 8440/1500 \approx 5.6\ \text{m/s}^2$. Using kinematics: $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2ad$ gives $0 = 225 - 2(5.6)(20) = 225 - 224$ ✓ (to rounding).

Example

A person of $70\ \text{kg}$ climbs stairs of $20\ \text{m}$ in $10\ \text{s}$. Find the work done against gravity and the average power. (From [1], Worked Example “Power”.)

  1. Work against gravity: $W = mgh = 70 \times 9.8 \times 20 \approx 1.37\times10^4\ \text{J}$.

  2. Average power: $P = W/\Delta t = 1.37\times10^4 / 10 \approx 1.37\ \text{kW}$.

Check

About $1.9\ \text{hp}$. A fit cyclist can sustain close to this ✓.
