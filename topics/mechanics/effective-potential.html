Effective Potential and Turning Points

For a particle in a central potential U(r), angular momentum conservation reduces the motion to one dimension in the effective potential U_eff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2μr²).

In short

For a central force (gravity, Coulomb attraction, spring pulling to a centre), angular momentum $L$ is conserved. That reduces the 2D problem to a 1D problem in a modified potential 2; 3.

The effective potential is 3:

$$U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \underbrace{\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}}_{\text{centrifugal barrier}}.$$

The extra bit is the centrifugal barrier. It acts like a wall at small $r$ because, by angular momentum conservation, the closer you get the faster you spin — and spinning fast takes energy.

Reading the graph of $U_{\text{eff}}$: pretend you're a particle sliding on it. You can only be where $E \ge U_{\text{eff}}(r)$:

  • Trapped in a dip? You oscillate back and forth between two $r$ values: a bound orbit, like a planet around the Sun (ellipse).
  • Sitting right at the bottom of the dip? A circular orbit.
  • Above the barrier? You come in, bounce off at some closest approach $r_{\min}$, and fly away: an unbound orbit (hyperbola — comets, scattering).

Falling in is hard. Unless $L = 0$ (purely radial motion), the centrifugal barrier always prevents you reaching $r = 0$. That's why the Earth doesn't spiral into the Sun 3.

Theory

Central motion: planar, conserving L

A particle of (reduced) mass $\mu$ in a central potential $U(r)$ has angular momentum $\mathbf L = \mu\,\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}$ conserved (central force → no torque) 1. Motion stays in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf L$. Use polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ in that plane. Then 2 §20.2:

$$L = \mu r^2\dot\theta = \text{const}.$$

Energy in polar form

The speed squared in polar coordinates is $\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2$. The energy is

$$E = \tfrac12\mu(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) + U(r).$$

Replace $\dot\theta = L/(\mu r^2)$ 2 §20.2:

$$E = \tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + \underbrace{\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2} + U(r)}_{U_{\text{eff}}(r)}.$$

The effective potential

The second term is the effective potential 2 §20.2; 3 §3.2:

$$\boxed{U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.}$$

The extra $L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ is the centrifugal barrier: a repulsive term that grows as $r \to 0$. It doesn't come from a real force — it comes from angular momentum conservation, which forces faster rotation at smaller $r$.

The radial motion now looks like 1D motion of a particle in $U_{\text{eff}}(r)$: $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 = E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)$ 3 §3.2.

Turning points and bound motion

Since $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 \ge 0$, the motion is restricted to $r$ with $E \ge U_{\text{eff}}(r)$. The boundaries where $E = U_{\text{eff}}$ are turning points: there $\dot r = 0$ instantaneously but $\dot\theta = L/(\mu r^2) \ne 0$, so the particle doesn't stop — it just reverses its radial motion 3 §3.2.

If there are two turning points $r_{\min}, r_{\max}$, the orbit is bound (oscillates between them). If there is only one ($r_{\min}$) and $U_{\text{eff}} \to 0$ at infinity, the orbit is unbound — the particle comes in from $\infty$, reaches the closest approach, and flies back out 2 §20.2; 3.

Shape of U_eff for inverse-square gravity

For $U(r) = -GMm/r$, the effective potential has a well 3 §3.2.1:

  • Deep well at intermediate $r$: minimum at $r = L^2/(GMm^2\mu)$.
  • Centrifugal barrier as $r \to 0^+$ — the particle can't fall to $r = 0$ unless $L = 0$.
  • Approaches 0 as $r \to \infty$.

Depending on the total energy $E$ 3 §3.2.1:

  • $E < 0$ and $E$ above the well's minimum: bound ellipse.
  • $E < 0$ exactly at the minimum: circular orbit.
  • $E = 0$: parabola, barely unbound.
  • $E > 0$: hyperbola.

Note

The centrifugal barrier is not a force in the lab frame — it comes from writing the radial equation while using angular momentum conservation. In the rotating frame of the orbit, it is a real centrifugal force (Topic 10.2) 3 §3.2.

Warning

$L$ is a property of the orbit — set by the initial conditions. Change $L$ and the whole $U_{\text{eff}}$ changes. Only for $L = 0$ does the particle move on a radial line and can reach $r = 0$ 3 §3.2.

Visualization

The idea with a different potential. Think of the central-force $U_{\text{eff}}(r)$: a well and a horizontal $E$ line. A particle with energy $E$ trapped in the well oscillates between the two turning points (perihelion and aphelion) while whirling around the centre — an ellipse. Raise $E$ above the well walls and it escapes. Here the $x$-axis plays the role of $r$ and the valley walls are from gravity's $-1/r$ (deepening it) plus the centrifugal $+L^2/r^2$ (lifting it near zero).

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

For the gravitational potential $U = -GMm/r$, find the radius $r_c$ of a circular orbit and the corresponding $L$ in terms of $r_c$. (From 3, §3.2.1.)

  1. $U_{\text{eff}} = -GMm/r + L^2/(2\mu r^2)$. Set $dU_{\text{eff}}/dr = 0$:

    $$\frac{GMm}{r^2} - \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3} = 0$$ $$\Rightarrow L^2 = GMm\mu r_c.$$

  2. With $\mu \approx m$ (Sun-planet): $L^2 = GMm^2 r_c$, so $L = m\sqrt{GMr_c}$.

Check

Orbital speed $v = L/(mr_c) = \sqrt{GM/r_c}$, matching Topic 7.2 ✓.

Example

For $U = -GMm/r$, find the two turning points of an elliptical orbit with energy $E$ and angular momentum $L$. (From 3, §3.2.1.)

  1. $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r) \Rightarrow E + GMm/r - L^2/(2\mu r^2) = 0$. Multiply by $r^2$:

    $$E r^2 + GMm\,r - \frac{L^2}{2\mu} = 0.$$

  2. Quadratic in $r$: $r_{\pm} = \dfrac{-GMm \pm \sqrt{(GMm)^2 + 2EL^2/\mu}}{2E}$.

  3. For $E < 0$ (bound) these give $r_{\min}$ (perihelion) and $r_{\max}$ (aphelion). Their average, $(r_+ + r_-)/2 = -GMm/(2E) = a$, is the semi-major axis — consistent with Topic 7.3.

Check

Sum of the roots: $r_+ + r_- = -GMm/E$ (from Vieta). So $a = -GMm/(2E)$, equivalent to $E = -GMm/(2a)$ ✓.

Example

For a repulsive inverse-square (like-charge Coulomb), $U(r) = k/r$ with $k > 0$. Show that $U_{\text{eff}}$ has no well: every orbit is unbound. (Classical scattering, 2 §20.4.)

  1. $U_{\text{eff}} = k/r + L^2/(2\mu r^2)$: both terms positive, both decrease as $r \to \infty$.

  2. $U_{\text{eff}}$ is monotonically decreasing for $r > 0$ (no minimum).

  3. So any $E > 0$ gives a single turning point $r_{\min}$ (closest approach) and the orbit is a hyperbola heading back out.

Check

This is exactly the setup for Rutherford scattering (Topic 8.4), where alpha particles come in, bend around the nucleus, and fly away ✓.
