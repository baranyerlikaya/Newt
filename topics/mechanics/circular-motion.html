Uniform Circular Motion

A body moving on a circle at constant speed: the velocity is always tangent, the acceleration always points to the centre with size v²/R, and some real force must supply it.

In short

Going around at constant speed. The speed stays the same, but the direction keeps changing — so the velocity vector keeps changing — so there must be an acceleration 1; 2.

Where does it point? Toward the centre. How big? 1; 2

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{R} = \omega^2 R.$$

And what makes it happen? By $\mathbf F = m\mathbf a$, some real force has to pull the thing toward the centre. That's the centripetal force $F_c = mv^2/R$ 3:

  • a string tension, for a stone on a string;
  • gravity, for the Moon;
  • friction, for a car on a flat curve;
  • the slope of a banked curve, for cars and trains.

Remove the force (let go of the string): the stone flies off tangent to the circle, not outward. That feeling of being "thrown outward" is just your body's wish to go straight, not a real outward push 3.

The period (time for one loop) is $T = 2\pi R/v = 2\pi/\omega$ 1.

In uniform circular motion, the centripetal force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work — the kinetic energy and speed never change 3.

Theory

Kinematics of uniform circular motion

A particle moves on a circle of radius $R$, with angular velocity $\omega$ constant. Then $\theta(t) = \omega t + \theta_0$ and the position is 1 §Circular Motion; 2 §6.2:

$$\mathbf r(t) = R\cos(\omega t + \theta_0)\,\hat\imath + R\sin(\omega t + \theta_0)\,\hat\jmath.$$

Its velocity is tangent to the circle, with constant speed 1; 2 §6.2.8:

$$v = R\omega.$$

Centripetal acceleration

Differentiate the velocity. The acceleration points from the particle toward the centre of the circle, with magnitude 1 §Circular Motion; 2 §6.3:

$$\boxed{a_c = R\omega^2 = \frac{v^2}{R}.}$$

This is the centripetal ("centre-seeking") acceleration. In vector form, $\mathbf a = -\omega^2\,\mathbf r$: it always points opposite to the position vector 1 Worked Example “Uniform Circular Motion”.

The force that causes it

By Newton's second law, this acceleration requires a centripetal force 3 §9.1:

$$F_c = m a_c = \frac{mv^2}{R}.$$

"Centripetal" names the direction, not the physical origin. The actual force that supplies it is one of the usual forces 3 §9.1:

  • tension in a string swinging a stone;
  • gravity holding a satellite in orbit;
  • friction between tyres and road in a car's turn;
  • the normal force on the inside of a banked track.

With no such force (release the string), the particle flies off tangentially, by Newton's first law.

Period and frequency

One trip around takes the period 1

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi R}{v}.$$

Tangential component: non-uniform circular motion

If the speed changes, the particle also has a tangential acceleration $a_t = R\alpha = dv/dt$, along the velocity. The total acceleration is the vector sum 2 §6.3:

$$\mathbf a = -R\omega^2\,\hat r + R\alpha\,\hat\theta, \qquad |\mathbf a| = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2}.$$

Uniform circular motion is the special case $\alpha = 0$: only the centripetal component is present, so $\mathbf a\perp\mathbf v$ and the speed stays constant.

Note

No work is done on the particle in uniform circular motion: the centripetal force is perpendicular to the velocity, so $P = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v = 0$ and the kinetic energy stays constant 3 §9.1.

Warning

There is no outward "centrifugal force" in an inertial frame — the feeling of being thrown outward in a car's turn is just your inertia pressing against the door. In a rotating (non-inertial) frame one adds a fictitious centrifugal force $+m\omega^2 r\,\hat r$; see Newton's Laws, Topic 3.1 3 §9.1.

Visualization

A particle on a circle of radius $r$ at constant $\omega$. The dark arrow is the velocity, always tangent to the circle, with magnitude $v = \omega r$. The grey arrow is the acceleration, always pointing to the centre, with magnitude $a = \omega^2 r = v^2/r$. Both values are shown live. The two arrows are always perpendicular, which is why the speed stays constant: no work is done.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball on a $1\ \text{m}$ string is swung in a horizontal circle at $2\ \text{m/s}$. Find the centripetal acceleration and the tension in the string. (Direct application of 3, §9.1.)

  1. $a_c = v^2/R = 4/1 = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  2. The tension is the only horizontal force, providing the centripetal force: $T = m a_c = 0.5 \times 4 = 2\ \text{N}$.

Check

Using $\omega = v/R = 2\ \text{rad/s}$: $a_c = R\omega^2 = 1 \times 4 = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$ ✓. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega = \pi \approx 3.14\ \text{s}$.

Example

A car of mass $1000\ \text{kg}$ takes a flat curve of radius $50\ \text{m}$ at $15\ \text{m/s}$. What is the minimum coefficient of static friction between tyres and road? (Application of 3, §9.1 with friction as the centripetal force.)

  1. The centripetal force must be $F_c = mv^2/R = 1000 \times 225/50 = 4500\ \text{N}$.

  2. Friction supplies this: $f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg$.

  3. Minimum: $\mu_s = v^2/(gR) = 225/(9.8 \times 50) \approx 0.46$.

Check

Independent of mass (it cancels). On an icy road, $\mu_s \approx 0.1$, so the safe speed drops to $v = \sqrt{\mu_s gR} \approx 7\ \text{m/s}$. ✓

Example

The Moon orbits the Earth in roughly a circle of radius $R_{e,m} = 3.84\times 10^8\ \text{m}$ with period $T \approx 27.3\ \text{days}$. What centripetal acceleration does it have? (From 3, §9.2.)

  1. Convert $T = 27.3 \times 86400\ \text{s} \approx 2.36\times 10^6\ \text{s}$, so $\omega = 2\pi/T \approx 2.66\times 10^{-6}\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. $a_c = R_{e,m}\omega^2 \approx 3.84\times 10^8 \times (2.66\times 10^{-6})^2 \approx 2.72\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Check

Gravity at the Moon's distance: $g_{\text{moon}} = GM_E/R_{e,m}^2 \approx 2.70\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2$, matching the centripetal acceleration. This is exactly Newton's observation that led to universal gravitation 3 §9.2. ✓
