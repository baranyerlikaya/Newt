Hyperbolic Orbits and Rutherford Scattering

Unbound central-force motion: a particle approaches from infinity with impact parameter b, bends in the force field, and leaves. The link between impact parameter and scattering angle, and the classic Rutherford formula.

In short

Flying past a force centre — a comet past the Sun, an alpha particle past a nucleus. Comes in from far away, bends, flies off in a different direction. The question: how much does it bend?

The two numbers that describe the incoming particle are 1:

  • Speed $v_0$ (while it's still far away).
  • Impact parameter $b$ (the perpendicular offset between its straight-line path and the force centre — if there were no force, how far it would miss by).

For inverse-square forces (gravity, Coulomb), the scattering angle $\chi$ obeys the simple formula 1:

$$\tan\frac{\chi}{2} = \frac{k}{\mu v_0^2 b}.$$

Small $b$ (close call): big deflection, maybe even backscatter. Large $b$: barely bends.

Rutherford's experiment (1909-1911): shoot alpha particles at gold foil. Most went straight through (large $b$). A small fraction bounced almost backward (tiny $b$). From the statistics, Rutherford computed how massive and how small the deflector must be — leading to the discovery of the atomic nucleus 1.

Closest approach: at the bend, the distance is $r_{\min}$. For a head-on hit at a repulsive force, $r_{\min} = k/E$ — simply where kinetic energy equals Coulomb potential energy 1.

Theory

Hyperbolic orbit

A particle approaches a central force from far away with speed $v_0$ at impact parameter $b$ (perpendicular offset of its initial line from the force centre). Its initial energy and angular momentum are 1 §20.2; 2 §3.5:

$$E = \tfrac12\mu v_0^2 > 0, \qquad L = \mu v_0 b.$$

$E > 0$ means the orbit is unbound; $e > 1$: it's a hyperbola.

Closest approach

At closest approach $r = r_{\min}$, $\dot r = 0$: all radial kinetic energy is zero. The turning-point condition $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r_{\min})$ gives 1 §20.2:

$$E = \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_{\min}^2} + U(r_{\min}).$$

For attractive gravity ($U = -k/r$) or repulsive Coulomb ($U = +k/r$), solve the resulting quadratic for $r_{\min}$ 1 §20.2, §20.4:

$$r_{\min} = \frac{k}{\mu v_0^2}\left[\pm 1 + \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\mu v_0^2\,b}{k}\right)^2}\right].$$

Upper sign for repulsive, lower for attractive.

The deflection angle

The scattering angle $\chi$ is the angle between the incoming and outgoing straight-line asymptotes 1 §20.3; 2 §3.5. For inverse-square force:

$$\tan\frac{\chi}{2} = \frac{k}{\mu v_0^2 b}.$$

Small $b$ (close hit) → large $\chi$ (strong deflection, even backscatter). Large $b$ (far miss) → small $\chi$ (gentle bend). Removing the force entirely ($k \to 0$): $\chi \to 0$.

Rutherford scattering

For two charges $Qq$ interacting through Coulomb repulsion, $k = Qq/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$, and the scattering angle formula above becomes 1 §20.4:

$$\tan\frac{\chi}{2} = \frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\mu v_0^2 b}.$$

Historically (Rutherford, 1911), this was derived classically for alpha particles scattering off a gold nucleus. It is still correct (up to quantum corrections) and it is how we learned that atoms have tiny, dense nuclei: the occasional large-angle scatter required the mass to be concentrated in a region much smaller than the atom itself 1 §20.4.

Differential cross section

Imagine a uniform beam of particles with impact parameters uniformly distributed. Particles with $b$ in $[b, b+db]$ scatter into angles in $[\chi, \chi+d\chi]$. The ratio $d\sigma/d\Omega = b\,|db/d\chi|/\sin\chi$ is the differential cross section. For Rutherford 2 §3.5:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left(\frac{k}{4E}\right)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\chi/2)}.$$

Diverges at small angles (tiny deflections are very common) and drops fast at large angles (big deflections rare) — exactly what Rutherford's Geiger-Marsden experiment measured.

Note

In a bound orbit (ellipse), the minimum $r$ is called perihelion (if Sun) or periastron. In an unbound orbit (hyperbola), it's called the closest approach $r_{\min}$. Same equation, different name — just whether $E$ is below or above zero 2.

Warning

The formula for $r_{\min}$ in the Theory section uses the reduced mass $\mu$. If one target is much heavier than the other (alpha particle on gold nucleus, or Sun on comet), $\mu \approx m_{\text{lighter}}$ and the heavy target can be treated as fixed 1 §20.2; 2 §3.5.

Visualization

Two bodies moving in straight lines (dashed) at constant velocities, like two particles too distant to feel each other. The graph shows the distance between them over time and marks its minimum. In a central-force problem, the attractive or repulsive interaction would bend the trajectories — the "straight-line" paths you see here are the "no-force" limit. The closest approach formula $r_{\min} \sim b$ holds when the force is weak; when it's strong (nearby charges, nuclear scattering), the real path bends far from these dashed lines.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A head-on collision ($b = 0$) between a $7.7\ \text{MeV}$ alpha particle ($z = 2$) and a gold nucleus ($Z = 79$). Find the closest approach. (From 1, §20.4 — the classic Rutherford estimate.)

  1. $b = 0$: $L = 0$, no centrifugal barrier. Full kinetic energy becomes Coulomb potential energy: $E = k/r_{\min}$.

  2. $k = zZ e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 2\times 79\times 1.44\ \text{MeV·fm} \approx 227.5\ \text{MeV·fm}$.

  3. $r_{\min} = k/E = 227.5/7.7 \approx 29.5\ \text{fm}$.

Check

About 30 fm — much smaller than an atom ($10^5\ \text{fm}$) but larger than a nuclear radius ($\sim 7\ \text{fm}$ for gold), consistent with Rutherford's conclusion that the alpha was repelled before hitting the nucleus ✓.

Example

What impact parameter gives $90°$ scattering for an alpha particle on gold at $5\ \text{MeV}$? (Application of scattering formula.)

  1. $\tan(45°) = 1 = k/(\mu v_0^2 b_{90})$, so $b_{90} = k/(2E) = k/(2E) = 227.5/(2\times 5) = 22.75\ \text{fm}$.

Check

Particles with impact parameters smaller than $b_{90}$ scatter through angles larger than $90°$ — a tiny target but a large cross section for near-backward scattering ✓.

Example

A comet approaches the Sun from far away with speed $v_0 = 30\ \text{km/s}$ at impact parameter $b = 10^{11}\ \text{m}$. Find its closest approach distance. ($GM_\odot = 1.32\times 10^{20}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$.)

  1. $k = GM_\odot m$ with $\mu \approx m$, so $k/\mu = GM_\odot$.

  2. Attractive closest approach: $r_{\min} = (GM_\odot/v_0^2)[-1 + \sqrt{1 + (v_0^2 b/GM_\odot)^2}]$.

  3. $GM_\odot/v_0^2 = 1.32\times 10^{20}/(3\times 10^4)^2 \approx 1.47\times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$.

  4. $(v_0^2 b/GM_\odot)^2 = (10^{11}/1.47\times 10^{11})^2 \approx 0.464$, so $\sqrt{1.464} \approx 1.21$.

  5. $r_{\min} \approx 1.47\times 10^{11}\times 0.21 \approx 3.1\times 10^{10}\ \text{m}$ — about 2/3 of Mercury's orbital distance.

Check

The comet is sucked in closer than its straight-line impact parameter would suggest, because gravity is attractive. The reverse happens for repulsive Coulomb ✓.
