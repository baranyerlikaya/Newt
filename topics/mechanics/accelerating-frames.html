Linearly Accelerating Frames

In a frame that accelerates linearly with A, the equation of motion picks up a fictitious force −mA. Everything else stays the same.

In short

In a car that speeds up, you feel pushed backward. Nothing is actually pushing you. Your body wants to keep its old speed (Newton's first law), but the seat is coming up faster than before.

If you insist on using the car as your reference frame (easier because the seat is at rest), you have to invent an extra "force" to explain why you're pressed back. That's the fictitious force 1:

$$\mathbf F_{\text{fic}} = -m\mathbf A,$$

where $\mathbf A$ is the car's acceleration. Opposite direction to the acceleration, and it looks exactly like gravity (same for every mass).

Then Newton's second law works again in the car's frame 1:

$$\mathbf F_{\text{real}} + \mathbf F_{\text{fic}} = m\mathbf a'.$$

Useful tricks:

  • A pendulum in an accelerating car hangs tilted. You can compute by just adding $-m\mathbf A$ to gravity.
  • In a free-falling lift, the fictitious force cancels gravity exactly — you feel weightless.
  • Astronauts in the ISS "float" because their frame is in free fall, not because gravity is absent.

Equivalence principle: gravity and acceleration are indistinguishable locally. Einstein built general relativity on this 1.

Theory

Setup

Let frame $O'$ accelerate linearly at $\mathbf A$ relative to an inertial frame $O$ (origins coincide at $t = 0$). Positions are related by 1 §31.2:

$$\mathbf r = \mathbf r' + \mathbf R(t), \qquad \ddot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf A.$$

Differentiating twice gives the accelerations 1:

$$\mathbf a = \mathbf a' + \mathbf A.$$

Newton's second law in the non-inertial frame

In the inertial frame: $m\mathbf a = \mathbf F_{\text{physical}}$. Substituting 1 §31.2:

$$m\mathbf a' = \mathbf F_{\text{physical}} - m\mathbf A.$$

Define the fictitious force

$$\mathbf F_{\text{fic}} = -m\mathbf A.$$

Then in the accelerating frame 1:

$$\boxed{\mathbf F_{\text{physical}} + \mathbf F_{\text{fic}} = m\mathbf a'.}$$

Same form as Newton's law, with an extra "pseudo-force" that points opposite to the frame's acceleration. Everything else carries through.

Why it's useful

Working in the accelerating frame is often easier because objects attached to the frame are at rest there. Pendulum in a car, water in a bucket — they look simple in the frame moving with the car.

The fictitious force has no reaction partner. You can't find the body "exerting" it. That's the dead giveaway that you are in a non-inertial frame 1.

Equivalence principle

The fictitious force is uniform, proportional to mass — exactly like gravity. An observer inside a sealed accelerating box can't tell whether the extra force is gravity or the box accelerating in the opposite direction. This is Einstein's equivalence principle, the starting point for general relativity 1 §31.2, Concept Question 1.

Example: in free fall (box accelerating at $g$ downward), the fictitious force cancels gravity exactly. You feel weightless. Same for astronauts in orbit.

Note

For systems in free fall together with a body (orbit, falling lift), the fictitious force exactly cancels gravity. That's why the body appears "weightless" in such a frame 1.

Warning

Fictitious forces are a consequence of the choice of frame, not of any physical interaction. Compute them from $-m\mathbf A$; don't look for a source 1.

Visualization

A pendulum inside a car accelerating to the right at $A$. In the car's frame the bob is at rest, with three forces balanced: gravity $mg$ downward, tension along the string, and the fictitious force $-mA$ backward. The string tilts backward by $\arctan(A/g)$ from the vertical. Set $A = 0$: fictitious force vanishes, string hangs straight down, car is an inertial frame.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A pendulum hangs from the roof of a car accelerating at $\mathbf A$. In equilibrium (as seen in the car), at what angle does the string hang? (From 1, Example 1.)

  1. In the car frame the bob is at rest. Forces: gravity $m\mathbf g$ down, tension along string, fictitious $-m\mathbf A$ backward.

  2. Equilibrium: tension cancels $m(\mathbf g - \mathbf A)$.

  3. The string lines up with $\mathbf g - \mathbf A$: tilted backward by $\arctan(A/g)$ from vertical.

Check

$A = 0$: hangs straight down ✓. Large $A$: nearly flat against the roof, pointing backward ✓.

Example

You're in a lift accelerating downward at $a < g$. What do you feel your weight is? (Standard application.)

  1. Lift frame: fictitious force $-m\mathbf A = +ma$ upward (since $\mathbf A$ points down).

  2. Effective gravity: $g_{\text{eff}} = g - a$ downward.

  3. You feel a weight $m(g-a) < mg$.

Check

Free fall ($a = g$): weightless ✓. Lift accelerating up ($a < 0$): heavier than normal, as expected ✓.

Example

Why do astronauts in the ISS float? (Conceptual, from 1, Concept Question 1 analogue.)

  1. The ISS is in a circular orbit — accelerating centripetally with $\mathbf A = -g_{\text{orb}}\hat r$ toward Earth.

  2. Inside the ISS frame, the fictitious force on an astronaut is $-m\mathbf A = +mg_{\text{orb}}\hat r$ outward.

  3. Earth's gravity provides $-mg_{\text{orb}}\hat r$ inward on the astronaut. They cancel exactly: $g_{\text{eff}} = 0$, hence weightless.

Check

Gravity isn't weak at the ISS (it's still ~89% of surface gravity). Astronauts float because the station is in free fall around Earth ✓.
