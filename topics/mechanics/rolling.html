Rolling Without Slipping

Rolling without slipping ties linear and angular motion together, V = ωR. Kinetic energy splits into translation + rotation, and different shapes roll down inclines at different speeds.

In short

Rolling without slipping means the point where the wheel touches the ground is not sliding. That gives 1:

$$V = \omega R.$$

The CM speed and the spin speed are locked together. The contact point is momentarily at rest.

Kinetic energy of a rolling body has two pieces 1:

  • Translation of the CM: $\tfrac12 MV^2$.
  • Rotation about the CM: $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$.

Shapes differ in how much is in each piece.

Famous ramp race: which shape rolls down fastest? 1

  1. Solid sphere ($I = \tfrac25 MR^2$) — fastest.
  2. Solid cylinder/disc ($I = \tfrac12 MR^2$).
  3. Hollow sphere ($I = \tfrac23 MR^2$).
  4. Thin hoop ($I = MR^2$) — slowest.

Why? For each shape, energy conservation gives $V = \sqrt{2gh/(1 + I/(MR^2))}$. More $I$ per $MR^2$ means more KE goes into spinning, less into going forward.

Mass and radius don't matter. A big heavy sphere and a tiny light one roll the same way. Only the shape matters.

Static friction is what makes it roll instead of slide — but it does no work, because the contact point isn't moving 1.

Theory

The constraint V = ωR

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping on a flat surface. The contact point between wheel and ground is instantaneously at rest (if it were moving, it would be slipping) 1 §20.2.1. For the CM moving at velocity $V$ while the wheel spins with $\omega$ about its axle:

$$V = \omega R.$$

Differentiating: $a_{\text{CM}} = \alpha R$ (linear and angular accelerations also tied).

Kinetic energy of a rolling body

Use the Koenig decomposition: total KE = CM translation + rotation about the CM 1 §20.4, §20.5:

$$K = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{CM}}\omega^2.$$

With the no-slip condition $V = \omega R$:

$$K = \tfrac12 MV^2\left(1 + \frac{I_{\text{CM}}}{MR^2}\right).$$

Each shape has its own $I_{\text{CM}}/MR^2$: $1/2$ for a disc, $1$ for a hoop, $2/5$ for a solid sphere, $2/3$ for a hollow sphere.

Rolling down a ramp

Starting from rest at height $h$, energy conservation 1:

$$Mgh = \tfrac12 MV^2\left(1 + \frac{I_{\text{CM}}}{MR^2}\right).$$

Solving for the speed at the bottom:

$$V = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + I_{\text{CM}}/(MR^2)}}.$$

The smaller $I_{\text{CM}}/(MR^2)$, the faster the body rolls — more energy goes into translation, less into rotation. So solid sphere > solid disc > hollow sphere > thin hoop — in that order of winning the race down the ramp. Mass and radius don't matter (they cancel).

Friction and rolling

Rolling without slipping needs a static friction force at the contact, which can be any value up to $\mu_s N$. This friction does no work (the contact point doesn't move), so it conserves mechanical energy even while it provides the torque that spins the wheel up 1 §20.2.1.

If the surface is too slippery, the body starts slipping: then kinetic friction applies and energy is lost to heat (that's why you lose energy skidding).

Moment-of-inertia equation on a slope

Instead of energy, use Newton + torque. Along the slope (angle $\theta$): $MA = Mg\sin\theta - f$. About the CM: $I_{\text{CM}}\alpha = fR$. With $A = \alpha R$: solve 1 §20.4:

$$A = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + I_{\text{CM}}/(MR^2)}.$$

Constant, so the body speeds up uniformly. Independent of $M$ and $R$, depends only on the shape (through the ratio $I_{\text{CM}}/(MR^2)$).

Note

The static friction on a rolling body is tiny when the slope is gentle. For $\theta$ above a critical angle (depending on $\mu_s$), the body starts to slip — then solid shapes still win, but by less, and energy is lost 1 §20.2.1.

Warning

$V = \omega R$ is a constraint, not a general identity. It only applies to rolling without slipping. For a wheel spinning in place (burning rubber) or sliding (icy road), $V$ and $\omega R$ aren't equal 1.

Visualization

A wheel rolls without slipping. CM moves forward at $v$, the wheel spins with $\omega = v/R$. The dot marks the instantaneous contact point — labelled "instant. at rest". Watch the dot: every time it touches the ground, its velocity is zero, then it lifts off and traces a cycloid. The whole rim does this, which is why the rim-point speed varies from $0$ at the bottom to $2v$ at the top.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A solid sphere and a hoop, both of mass $M$ and radius $R$, roll from rest down the same ramp of height $h$. Who wins? And what are the speeds at the bottom? (From 1, §20.4–20.5.)

  1. Sphere: $I/(MR^2) = 2/5$, so $V_{\text{sph}} = \sqrt{2gh/(1 + 2/5)} = \sqrt{10gh/7}$.

  2. Hoop: $I/(MR^2) = 1$, so $V_{\text{hoop}} = \sqrt{2gh/(1 + 1)} = \sqrt{gh}$.

  3. Ratio: $V_{\text{sph}}/V_{\text{hoop}} = \sqrt{10/7} \approx 1.20$. The sphere is about 20% faster.

Check

Independent of mass and radius ✓. A big billiard ball and a small marble both roll the same way down the same slope; a bike wheel and a solid wheel of the same mass and radius do not.

Example

What is the acceleration of a solid cylinder rolling without slipping down a $30°$ slope? (Direct application.)

  1. Solid cylinder: $I/(MR^2) = 1/2$.

  2. $A = g\sin30°/(1 + 1/2) = (9.81\times 0.5)/1.5 \approx 3.27\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  3. A frictionless block would have $A = g\sin30° = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$ — faster, because it doesn't have to spin up any rotational KE.

Check

Two-thirds of the "no-rotation" acceleration ✓. For a hoop it would be exactly half.

Example

Show that the static friction on a solid sphere rolling down a ramp is $f = \tfrac{2}{7}Mg\sin\theta$. (From 1, §20.4.)

  1. From the two equations: $MA = Mg\sin\theta - f$ and $I_{\text{CM}}\alpha = fR$ with $I_{\text{CM}} = \tfrac25 MR^2$ and $A = \alpha R$: get $f = \tfrac25 MA = \tfrac25 M \cdot g\sin\theta/(1 + 2/5) = \tfrac{2}{7}Mg\sin\theta$.

  2. Friction scales with $\sin\theta$. No-slip holds while $f < \mu_s N = \mu_s Mg\cos\theta$, i.e. while $\tan\theta < \tfrac72\mu_s$. Above that, the sphere slips.

Check

For $\mu_s = 0.3$: $\theta_{\text{slip}} = \arctan(7\times 0.3/2) = \arctan(1.05) \approx 46°$. Steeper slopes make a ball slide instead of rolling ✓.
