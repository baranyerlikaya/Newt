Collision Theory

Momentum is always conserved in collisions. Kinetic energy may or may not be. Elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic cases, in both the lab and CM frames.

In short

In every collision, total momentum is conserved — the external forces during the brief contact are negligible compared to the internal forces 1.

Three flavours by what happens to the kinetic energy:

  • Elastic: KE conserved. Billiard balls, atomic nuclei.
  • Inelastic: KE partly lost to heat/deformation. Most everyday collisions.
  • Perfectly inelastic: bodies stick together. Maximum possible energy loss.

1D elastic formulas (body 1 hits stationary body 2) 1:

$$v_1' = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}v_1, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}v_1.$$

Three cases:

  • Equal masses: body 1 stops, body 2 takes over its velocity. Newton's cradle.
  • Light hits heavy: light bounces back, heavy barely moves. Ball off a wall.
  • Heavy hits light: heavy continues, light flies off fast. Bowling pin.

Perfectly inelastic: stuck-together speed is the centre-of-mass velocity, which never changed anyway. The relative kinetic energy is what gets lost 1.

Trick: go to the CM frame. There the total momentum is zero, and an elastic collision just reverses each body's velocity. Transform back by adding $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}}$ 1.

Theory

The three types

Classify by what happens to kinetic energy 1 §15.3; 2:

  • Elastic: $\Delta K = 0$. Momentum and KE both conserved.
  • Inelastic: $\Delta K < 0$. Some energy becomes heat, sound, deformation.
  • Perfectly inelastic: the bodies stick together. Maximum $K$ loss consistent with momentum conservation.

Momentum is always conserved (assuming the external impulse during the brief collision is negligible) 1 §15.3.

1D elastic collision

Two bodies $m_1, m_2$ with initial velocities $v_1, v_2$ and final $v_1', v_2'$. Momentum and energy 1 §15.4.1:

$$m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = m_1v_1' + m_2v_2',$$ $$\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2 = \tfrac12 m_1v_1'^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2'^2.$$

Solving simultaneously 1:

$$v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_1 + \frac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_2,$$ $$v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_1 + \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_2.$$

Equivalent handy result: relative velocity reverses: $v_1' - v_2' = -(v_1 - v_2)$ 1.

Special cases of elastic 1D

  • Equal masses, body 2 at rest: body 1 stops, body 2 moves off at $v_1$ (Newton's cradle).
  • Light hits heavy, body 2 at rest ($m_1 \ll m_2$): body 1 bounces back nearly reversed, body 2 barely moves. Ball off a wall.
  • Heavy hits light, body 2 at rest ($m_1 \gg m_2$): body 1 continues almost unchanged, body 2 flies off at $\approx 2v_1$. Bowling pin.

Perfectly inelastic

Bodies stick together: $v_1' = v_2' = v_f$. Momentum: $m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = (m_1 + m_2)v_f$, so 1 §15.3

$$v_f = \frac{m_1v_1 + m_2v_2}{m_1 + m_2} = V_{\text{CM}}.$$

The combined body moves at the CM velocity (necessary, since that's never changed anyway). Energy loss 1:

$$\Delta K = -\tfrac12\mu(v_1 - v_2)^2, \qquad \mu = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$

All of the KE that was in relative motion is lost; the CM KE is kept.

CM frame makes it trivial

In the CM frame the total momentum is zero: $m_1\mathbf u_1 + m_2\mathbf u_2 = 0$. An elastic collision there is just a reversal of each velocity (for 1D) or a rotation of each (for 2D) while keeping magnitudes fixed 1 §15.2, §15.7. Transform back to the lab frame by adding $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}}$.

2D collisions have an extra degree of freedom (scattering angle $\chi$): momentum conservation + energy conservation gives 3 equations in 4 unknowns $(v_1', v_2', \theta_1', \theta_2')$. The scattering angle is set by the impact parameter — a geometric detail of how the two bodies touched 1 §15.6, §15.7.

Note

"Elastic" doesn't mean rubbery — it means no mechanical energy is lost. Billiard balls, atomic nuclei at low energy and hard spheres are elastic to good approximation. Mud balls, cars, and skaters are not 1 §15.3.

Warning

Momentum conservation is a vector statement. In two dimensions you get two equations; in three, three. Energy is one scalar equation. You can't conserve more unknowns than you have equations — that's why 2D elastic collisions still leave a free scattering angle 1 §15.6.

Visualization

A perfectly inelastic collision: cart 1 (dark, $m_1$) hits stationary cart 2 (grey, $m_2$) and they stick. Momentum conservation: $m_1v_1 = (m_1+m_2)v_f$. The bars show $p_1$, $p_2$ and the sum: the sum never changes, but $p_1$ decreases and $p_2$ grows during the collision. For elastic collisions, both bars would still conserve, but the velocities would differ — equal masses give a perfect stop-and-swap, unequal masses give a bounce.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

$m_1 = 1\ \text{kg}$ at $v_1 = 3\ \text{m/s}$ hits stationary $m_2 = 3\ \text{kg}$ elastically. Find the final velocities. (From 1, §15.4.1.)

  1. $v_1' = (1-3)\cdot 3/(1+3) = -1.5\ \text{m/s}$ (bounces back).

  2. $v_2' = 2\cdot 1\cdot 3/(1+3) = 1.5\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

Momentum: $3 = 1\cdot(-1.5) + 3\cdot 1.5 = -1.5 + 4.5 = 3$ ✓. Energy: $\tfrac12(3)^2 = 4.5$ initial, $\tfrac12(1)(1.5^2) + \tfrac12(3)(1.5^2) = 1.125 + 3.375 = 4.5$ final ✓. Relative velocity: before $3-0 = 3$, after $-1.5 - 1.5 = -3$, reversed ✓.

Example

Same bodies but perfectly inelastic. Find $v_f$ and the fraction of KE lost. (From 1, §15.3.)

  1. $v_f = (1\cdot 3 + 3\cdot 0)/4 = 0.75\ \text{m/s}$.

  2. $K_i = 4.5\ \text{J}$, $K_f = \tfrac12(4)(0.75^2) = 1.125\ \text{J}$.

  3. Fraction lost: $(4.5 - 1.125)/4.5 = 75\%$.

Check

Verify with $\mu$: $\mu = 1\cdot 3/4 = 0.75$, $\Delta K = -\tfrac12(0.75)(3)^2 = -3.375\ \text{J}$ ✓.

Example

A $5\ \text{g}$ bullet moving at $400\ \text{m/s}$ embeds in a $2\ \text{kg}$ block of wood resting on a frictionless table. Find the block's speed and the fraction of KE converted to heat. (Standard ballistic pendulum setup, from 1, §15.3.)

  1. Perfectly inelastic: $v_f = (0.005\times 400)/(0.005 + 2) = 2/2.005 \approx 0.998\ \text{m/s}$.

  2. $K_i = \tfrac12(0.005)(400)^2 = 400\ \text{J}$. $K_f = \tfrac12(2.005)(0.998)^2 \approx 1.0\ \text{J}$.

  3. Fraction lost: $(400-1)/400 \approx 99.75\%$. Nearly all the KE becomes heat and deformation.

Check

This is why bullets are so destructive: a huge fraction of their KE deposits in the target ✓.
