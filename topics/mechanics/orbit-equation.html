Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits

Combining energy conservation and angular momentum gives the shape r(θ) of the orbit. For the inverse-square force this is a conic section.

In short

For a central force, $r(\theta)$ describes the shape of the orbit — the map in space, time removed 1.

For gravity (and any inverse-square force), the shape is always a conic section with the force centre at one focus:

$$r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}.$$

The eccentricity $e$ picks the shape:

  • $e = 0$: perfect circle.
  • $0 < e < 1$: ellipse (Earth, Mars, all planets; comets like Halley).
  • $e = 1$: parabola (marginal, just barely escapes).
  • $e > 1$: hyperbola (fly-bys, scattering, interstellar comets).

The energy decides between bound and unbound: $E < 0$ gives ellipse, $E = 0$ parabola, $E > 0$ hyperbola.

Semi-major axis $a$ holds the energy: all ellipses with the same $a$ have the same energy and the same period (Kepler 3) — regardless of their shape (eccentricity) 1.

Theory

From dθ/dr to r(θ)

Angular momentum gives $\dot\theta = L/(\mu r^2)$, and the radial energy equation gives $\dot r = \pm\sqrt{(2/\mu)[E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)]}$. Dividing 1:

$$\frac{d\theta}{dr} = \frac{L/(\mu r^2)}{\pm\sqrt{(2/\mu)[E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)]}}.$$

Integrating gives the orbit $\theta(r)$ or, inverted, $r(\theta)$ — the shape of the path in space, with time removed.

Substitution u = 1/r

For $U = -k/r$ (inverse-square attraction, with $k = GMm$ for gravity), substitute $u = 1/r$ 1:

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}.$$

A linear ODE with the same shape as SHM. Its solution is

$$u(\theta) = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}\left 1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)\right,$$

where $e$ is an integration constant. Inverting 1:

$$r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)}, \qquad p = \frac{L^2}{\mu k}.$$

This is a conic section with the force centre at one focus.

Kepler orbits: the four conics

The eccentricity $e$ decides the shape 1:

  • $e = 0$: circle, $r = p$ constant.
  • $0 < e < 1$: ellipse, bound orbit. Periastron $r_{\min} = p/(1+e)$, apastron $r_{\max} = p/(1-e)$. Semi-major axis $a = p/(1-e^2)$.
  • $e = 1$: parabola, $E = 0$, barely escaping.
  • $e > 1$: hyperbola, $E > 0$, scattering trajectory.

In terms of energy and angular momentum 1:

$$e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2}}.$$

Energy and semi-major axis

For bound orbits ($E < 0$) one finds (as in Topic 7.3):

$$a = -\frac{k}{2E}, \qquad E = -\frac{k}{2a}.$$

So the orbit's energy depends only on $a$, not $L$ or $e$. All orbits with the same semi-major axis have the same energy and the same period (Kepler's third law).

Note

The orbit closes up (comes back to exactly the same path) only for certain special force laws. The inverse-square force is one of them — perfect ellipses. Any small deviation from inverse-square causes the ellipse to precess, slowly rotating in space. General relativity predicts such a precession for Mercury's orbit 1.

Warning

The orbit equation gives the shape $r(\theta)$, not $r(t)$ or $\theta(t)$. To get motion in time, you still need Kepler's second law: $dA/dt = L/(2\mu) = \text{const}$, which gives $\theta(t)$ implicitly as an integral 1.

Visualization

An orbit around a central mass (dark dot at the focus, not the centre). Change the eccentricity $e$: a circle at $e = 0$, elongated ellipses as $e$ grows. In every case the shaded wedge swept in a fixed time is the same area (Kepler 2), so the particle moves faster when it's close and slower when it's far.

Worked examples

Example

Identify the orbit shapes for the Earth, a parabolic comet, and an interstellar visitor like 'Oumuamua. (Standard application of eccentricity and $E$.)

  1. Earth: $e \approx 0.017$, $E < 0$, nearly circular ellipse.

  2. Typical periodic comet (Halley): $e \approx 0.97$, $E < 0$, highly elongated ellipse.

  3. 'Oumuamua: $e \approx 1.2$, $E > 0$, unbound hyperbola — it came from outside the solar system and is leaving.

Check

Each eccentricity directly corresponds to a shape. $e < 1$: closed, periodic. $e \ge 1$: open, one-pass ✓.

Example

Earth's orbit has perihelion $r_{\min} = 1.47\times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ and aphelion $r_{\max} = 1.52\times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$. Find $a$ and $e$. (Standard.)

  1. $a = (r_{\min} + r_{\max})/2 = 1.495\times 10^{11}\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{AU}$.

  2. $e = (r_{\max} - r_{\min})/(r_{\max} + r_{\min}) = 0.05\times 10^{11}/(2.99\times 10^{11}) \approx 0.0167$.

Check

Earth's semi-major axis defines the astronomical unit by convention; its eccentricity is about 0.017 ✓.

Example

A comet is observed with $E = 0$ (parabolic). Find its closest approach to the Sun in terms of its angular momentum. (Direct from orbit equation.)

  1. $e = 1$ for $E = 0$. Orbit: $r = p/(1+\cos\theta)$.

  2. Closest approach at $\theta = 0$: $r_{\min} = p/2 = L^2/(2\mu k) = L^2/(2\mu GMm)$.

  3. With $\mu \approx m$: $r_{\min} = L^2/(2GMm^2) = v_{\min}^2 r_{\min}^2/(2GM)$, so $v_{\min}^2 = 2GM/r_{\min}$ — exactly escape velocity at perihelion ✓.

Check

A parabolic orbit is the marginal one between bound (ellipse) and free (hyperbola): its speed at every point is exactly local escape velocity ✓.
