Torque, Angular Momentum

Angular momentum L = r × p and torque τ = r × F, and the rotational form of Newton's second law dL/dt = τ.

In short

Rotation needs its own "momentum" and its own "force":

  • Angular momentum $\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$: how much a thing's motion goes around a chosen point. Bigger if it's far from the point, or moving fast perpendicular to the line to it 1; 2.
  • Torque $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r\times\mathbf F$: how much a force twists a thing about a chosen point. A long wrench gives you more torque with the same hand force — moment arm matters 1; 3.

Rule for direction: curl your right-hand fingers from $\mathbf r$ to the second vector; your thumb points along $\mathbf L$ or $\boldsymbol\tau$.

The rotational Newton's second law: torque changes angular momentum at exactly the rate of the torque 1; 2:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt} = \boldsymbol\tau.}$$

That's the same shape as $d\mathbf p/dt = \mathbf F$, just rotational.

No torque? Angular momentum is conserved. A skater pulls in their arms → smaller "spread", smaller $I$ → spins faster, because $L$ stays the same 1; 2.

Pick the pivot cleverly. Forces passing through a chosen point give no torque about it. Choosing the pivot of a hinge usually kills the unknown hinge force 3.

Theory

Angular momentum of a particle

The angular momentum of a particle about a reference point $O$ is 1 §Angular-Momentum Form of the 2nd Axiom; 2 §19.2

$$\mathbf L = \mathbf r \times \mathbf p,$$

where $\mathbf r$ is the particle's position relative to $O$ and $\mathbf p = m\mathbf v$ its momentum. Its magnitude is $|\mathbf L| = rp\sin\phi = r p_\perp = p r_\perp$, where $\phi$ is the angle between $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf p$ 2 §19.2. Units: kg·m²/s.

By the right-hand rule, $\mathbf L$ is perpendicular to the plane spanned by $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf p$ 2 §19.2.2.

Torque of a force

The torque of a force $\mathbf F$ about the same point $O$ is 1; 3 §17.3

$$\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F.$$

Its magnitude $|\boldsymbol\tau| = rF\sin\phi$ depends on the force, the distance from the pivot, and the angle between $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf F$. Units: N·m. A force pointing along $\mathbf r$ (towards or away from the pivot) produces no torque 3 §17.3.

The moment arm is $r_\perp = r\sin\phi$, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. Then $|\boldsymbol\tau| = r_\perp F$ 3 §17.3.

Rotational second law: dL/dt = τ

Differentiate $\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ by the product rule 1:

$$\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p + \mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf p}.$$

For constant mass, $\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p = \mathbf v\times(m\mathbf v) = 0$. And by Newton's second law, $\dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf F$. So 1; 2 §19.3

$$\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt} = \boldsymbol\tau.}$$

This is the rotational analogue of $d\mathbf p/dt = \mathbf F$: a torque changes angular momentum at the rate given by the torque 1.

The analogy

Translation and rotation about a fixed point run in parallel 1 §Angular-Momentum Form of the 2nd Axiom:

  • $\mathbf p$ (linear momentum) ↔ $\mathbf L$ (angular momentum)
  • $\mathbf F$ (force) ↔ $\boldsymbol\tau$ (torque)
  • $\mathbf F = d\mathbf p/dt$ ↔ $\boldsymbol\tau = d\mathbf L/dt$

If the net torque about $O$ is zero, $\mathbf L$ about $O$ is conserved; this is the rotational version of momentum conservation 1 §Conservation of Angular Momentum; 2 §19.4.

Choice of reference point

Both $\mathbf L$ and $\boldsymbol\tau$ depend on the chosen reference point $O$. Choose $O$ wisely: pick a point where some forces have no torque (e.g. on the line of action, so $\mathbf r\times\mathbf F = 0$), to simplify the equation 3 §17.3. Common choices are the pivot of a hinge or the centre of a circle.

Note

$\mathbf L$ is defined even for a particle moving in a straight line. If the line misses the origin by a perpendicular distance $b$, then $|\mathbf L| = mvb$, and $\mathbf L$ is constant because no force (hence no torque) acts 2 §19.2.

Warning

$\boldsymbol\tau$ and $\mathbf L$ about different points are different vectors. When you write $\boldsymbol\tau = d\mathbf L/dt$, both must be taken about the same point 3 §17.3.

Worked examples

Example

A particle of mass $m$ moves in a circle of radius $R$ with speed $v$. Find the magnitude of its angular momentum about the centre. (From 2, §19.2.)

  1. $\mathbf r$ points from the centre to the particle; $\mathbf v$ is tangent, so $\mathbf r\perp\mathbf v$ and $\phi = 90^\circ$.

  2. $|\mathbf L| = r\,p\sin90^\circ = R\,mv$.

  3. Using $v = R\omega$: $L = mR^2\omega$.

Check

$\mathbf L$ points along the axis (out of the page for counterclockwise motion). Its magnitude is positive and constant for uniform circular motion ✓.

Example

A force $\mathbf F = 3\hat\jmath\ \text{N}$ is applied at position $\mathbf r = (2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath)\ \text{m}$. Find the torque about the origin. (From the course's vector worked example 4 Example 4; also 3 §17.3.)

  1. $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r\times\mathbf F = (2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath)\times 3\hat\jmath$.

  2. Expand: $6(\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath) + 3(\hat\jmath\times\hat\jmath) = 6\hat k + 0$.

  3. So $\boldsymbol\tau = 6\hat k\ \text{N·m}$, pointing out of the $xy$-plane.

Check

The $\hat\jmath$-component of $\mathbf r$ is parallel to $\mathbf F$ and gives no torque. Only the $2\hat\imath$ component matters, giving $2 \times 3 = 6$ N·m. ✓

Example

A pendulum bob of mass $m$ swings on a string of length $L$. About the pivot, find the angular momentum and torque at the angle $\theta$ from vertical, when the bob has tangential speed $v$. Show $\dot L = \tau$ gives the pendulum equation. (Adapted from 1, with the pendulum setup from 5, §Simple pendulum.)

  1. Position: $\mathbf r$ points from the pivot down and outward along the string, $|\mathbf r| = L$. Momentum: tangential, magnitude $mv$, perpendicular to $\mathbf r$.

  2. $|\mathbf L| = mvL = mL^2\dot\theta$, perpendicular to the plane of swing.

  3. Only gravity gives a torque. Component perpendicular to $\mathbf r$: $mg\sin\theta$, moment arm $L$: $|\boldsymbol\tau| = mgL\sin\theta$ (restoring).

  4. $\dot L = \tau$: $mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta$, i.e. $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\sin\theta = 0$.

Check

For small $\theta$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$: $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\theta = 0$, the simple-harmonic pendulum equation with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$. ✓
