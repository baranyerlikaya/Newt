Variable Mass: the Rocket Equation

A rocket loses mass by ejecting exhaust. In gravity-free space, momentum conservation gives Tsiolkovsky's equation Δv = u ln(m₀/m).

In short

A rocket shoots out exhaust backward and gains momentum forward, by Newton's third law. The hard part is the mass keeps changing.

The rocket equation — one of the most important in aerospace 1:

$$\Delta v = u\,\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m}.$$
  • $u$: exhaust speed (how fast the gas leaves, in the rocket's frame).
  • $m_0$: mass before the burn.
  • $m$: mass after.

The log is cruel. To double $\Delta v$ you must square the mass ratio. To get $\Delta v = 2u$, $m_0/m = e^2 \approx 7.4$: you burn $87\%$ of your mass. For $\Delta v = 3u$, you burn $95\%$.

That's why rockets are mostly fuel — think of Saturn V, almost entirely propellant with a tiny capsule on top 1.

Going up Earth's gravity well costs an extra $gt_b$ (the "gravity loss"). The faster you burn, the less you pay. Fast burns mean high thrust engines 1.

Staging — dropping empty tanks — helps because the log grows slowly: a lighter rocket needs less fuel to accelerate. Every real orbital rocket has multiple stages.

Ion engines trade thrust for exhaust speed: $u$ ten times bigger means a much better mass ratio, but a flea's push. Good for deep-space probes, not for lift-off.

Theory

The problem of variable mass

A rocket isn't just one lump of matter — it continuously spits out hot gas. $F = ma$ for the rocket alone is wrong, because the system is losing mass. The right place to apply Newton's laws is to the whole (rocket + exhaust so far), or to apply momentum conservation directly 1 §12.3.

Derivation of the rocket equation

Rocket of mass $m$ moves at velocity $v$. In a short time $dt$ it ejects a tiny bit of mass $|dm|$ ($dm < 0$) at velocity $v - u$ (where $u > 0$ is the exhaust speed relative to the rocket) 1 §12.3.1.

Momentum conservation (no external force):

$$mv = (m + dm)(v + dv) + (-dm)(v - u).$$

Expanding and keeping first-order terms gives the rocket equation 1:

$$m\,dv = -u\,dm \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dv = -u\,\frac{dm}{m}.$$

Tsiolkovsky's equation

Integrate from initial mass $m_0$ and velocity $v_0 = 0$ to current mass $m$ and velocity $v$ 1 §12.3.1:

$$\boxed{\Delta v = u\,\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m}.}$$

This is Konstantin Tsiolkovsky's equation (1903). The rocket gains velocity in proportion to the exhaust speed and the log of the mass ratio. Logarithm is harsh: to double $\Delta v$ you must square the mass ratio.

With gravity

In a constant gravitational field, the exhaust still conserves momentum in the local frame, but gravity decelerates the rocket. If the rocket burns fuel at a rate $|\dot m|$ for time $t_b$ 1 §12.3.2:

$$\Delta v = u\,\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m} - g t_b.$$

The "gravity loss" term $gt_b$ punishes slow burns: faster thrust is more efficient when fighting gravity.

Staging

Because Tsiolkovsky's equation is logarithmic, carrying unneeded empty tanks is costly. By dropping empty stages, each remaining stage starts with a smaller $m_0$ but also a much smaller $m$, so $\Delta v$ accumulates more efficiently. Single-stage-to-orbit is extremely hard; multi-stage rockets (Saturn V, Falcon 9) are the standard 1 §12.3.

Note

Exhaust speed $u$ is the key design parameter. Chemical rockets: $u \sim 3\ \text{km/s}$ (kerosene/LOX) to $4.4\ \text{km/s}$ (LH₂/LOX). Ion thrusters: $u \sim 30\ \text{km/s}$ — great efficiency but tiny thrust. Nuclear or fusion rockets would reach even higher 1.

Warning

$u$ is the exhaust velocity relative to the rocket, not relative to the ground. A rocket moving at $5\ \text{km/s}$ with $u = 3\ \text{km/s}$ ejects gas moving backward at $2\ \text{km/s}$ in the ground frame 1.

Visualization

A simplified "rocket" picture. Cart 1 (dark, mass $m_1$) is the exhaust, cart 2 (grey, mass $m_2$) is the rocket. Momentum conservation: $m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = \text{const}$, so if $m_1$ is kicked backward, $m_2$ gets pushed forward. Change $m_1$ and $m_2$ to see the mass-ratio effect. The real rocket equation is this idea repeated continuously over a long burn.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A rocket with exhaust speed $u = 3\ \text{km/s}$ burns $80\%$ of its mass as fuel. Find $\Delta v$ (ignoring gravity). (Standard application of Tsiolkovsky's equation.)

  1. $m/m_0 = 0.2$, so $\ln(m_0/m) = \ln 5 \approx 1.61$.

  2. $\Delta v = 3\times 1.61 \approx 4.83\ \text{km/s}$.

Check

Less than one Earth orbital velocity (${\sim}8\ \text{km/s}$). To reach orbit from the ground requires a mass fraction of fuel even higher, plus staging, plus overcoming gravity ✓.

Example

To reach Earth orbital velocity $\Delta v = 8\ \text{km/s}$ in gravity-free space with chemical exhaust $u = 3\ \text{km/s}$, what mass fraction must be fuel? (Standard application.)

  1. $\Delta v/u = 8/3 \approx 2.67 = \ln(m_0/m)$, so $m_0/m \approx e^{2.67} \approx 14.4$.

  2. Fuel fraction: $1 - m/m_0 \approx 1 - 1/14.4 \approx 93\%$.

Check

And this ignores gravity losses. Real-world Falcon 9: first-stage fuel fraction $\sim 95\%$ ✓.

Example

Thrust from a $500\ \text{kg/s}$ burn at $u = 4\ \text{km/s}$. (Direct $F = \dot m\,u$.)

  1. $F = |\dot m|u = 500 \times 4000 = 2.0\times 10^6\ \text{N} = 2\ \text{MN}$.

Check

Consistent with upper-stage rocket engines (a Merlin Vacuum produces about 1 MN) ✓.
