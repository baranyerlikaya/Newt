Driven Oscillations and Resonance

Shake a damped oscillator at frequency ω and after a transient it settles into steady-state motion at the same frequency. The amplitude peaks when ω is near ω₀: resonance.

In short

Shake a damped oscillator at frequency $\omega$ and it does something interesting 1:

  1. At first: a confusing mix (its own natural wobble + the drive). This is the transient.
  2. After a while: the natural wobble dies off, and the oscillator settles into motion at the driving frequency $\omega$, with some amplitude $A$ and some phase lag behind the force.

The amplitude depends on the driving frequency, and peaks when $\omega$ is near the natural $\omega_0$ — that's resonance 1:

$$A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega)^2}}.$$

Three cases to remember:

  • Very slow drive: the mass just follows. $A = F_0/k$, same as pushing it statically.
  • Drive at $\omega_0$ (resonance): amplitude is boosted by the quality factor $Q$ (Topic 6.3). $A = Q \cdot F_0/k$.
  • Very fast drive: the mass can't keep up. Tiny amplitude.

Why it matters:

  • Pushing a swing works because you push at the swing's natural rhythm.
  • Radios tune by matching a circuit's resonance to one station's frequency.
  • Earthquakes damage buildings whose natural frequency matches the shake.
  • Tuning a guitar: pluck one string and the others at the same note hum sympathetically.

Weakly damped systems resonate sharply (high $Q$, narrow peak). Heavily damped systems don't really resonate at all.

Theory

The equation

Add a sinusoidal driving force $F(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t)$ to a damped oscillator 1 §Driven Oscillations:

$$m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = F_0\cos(\omega t),$$ $$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega t).$$

This is a nonhomogeneous linear ODE. Its general solution is a transient (the damped-oscillator solution, which decays) plus a steady-state particular solution 1:

$$x(t) = x_c(t) + x_p(t), \qquad \lim_{t\to\infty}x_c(t) = 0.$$

Steady-state solution

In the long run (after a few $1/\gamma$) only the particular solution survives, oscillating at the driving frequency $\omega$ 1:

$$x_p(t) = A\cos(\omega t - \delta).$$

Switching to the complex plane ($z_p = A e^{i(\omega t-\delta)}$) and substituting into the complex-extended equation gives 1:

$$A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega)^2}},$$ $$\tan\delta = \frac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}.$$

Three regimes of ω

Reading the amplitude and phase 1 §How to read amplitude and phase equations:

  • Slow drive ($\omega \ll \omega_0$): $A \approx F_0/k$ (the static deflection), $\delta \approx 0$ — the mass follows the force in phase.
  • At resonance ($\omega = \omega_0$): $A = F_0/(2m\gamma\omega_0) = Q\,F_0/k$, amplified by $Q$. $\delta = \pi/2$: the mass lags the force by a quarter period.
  • Fast drive ($\omega \gg \omega_0$): $A \approx F_0/(m\omega^2) \to 0$ — the mass can't keep up. $\delta \to \pi$: out of phase.

Resonance frequency

The amplitude peaks at 1:

$$\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2}.$$

Slightly below $\omega_0$. For weak damping ($Q \gg 1$) the shift is tiny and one usually says "resonance is at $\omega_0$". The amplitude at the peak grows without bound as $\gamma \to 0$: an undamped oscillator driven on resonance accumulates energy forever 1.

The width of the resonance peak (full width at half max of $A^2$) is about $\omega_0/Q$: high $Q$ gives a sharp peak, low $Q$ a broad one.

Everyday resonance

Resonance explains much 1:

  • Pushing a child on a swing at the right rhythm.
  • Smashing a wine glass with a note at its resonant frequency.
  • Radio tuning: an $RLC$ circuit's resonance picks one station.
  • Tacoma Narrows Bridge: wind coupling to a torsional mode at its natural frequency.

Note

The transient part of the response contains the natural frequency $\omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$; the steady-state part has the driving frequency $\omega$. Early on you hear beats between them, then the natural part fades 1.

Warning

Resonance only amplifies weakly damped systems. If $\gamma \gtrsim \omega_0$, there is no peak — the response just drops with $\omega$. $Q > 1$ is needed for a visible resonance 1.

Visualization

The curve $A(\omega)$ for a driven damped oscillator with $\omega_0 = 1$. The amplitude is small for slow drives, peaks near $\omega_0$, and dies off for fast drives. The peak height is about $Q \cdot F_0/k$ where $Q = 1/(2\gamma)$. Reduce $\gamma$ (less damping): the peak grows and sharpens. Increase $\gamma$: the peak lowers and flattens.

Worked examples

Example

$m = 1\ \text{kg}$, $k = 100\ \text{N/m}$, $\gamma = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$, driven by $F_0 = 1\ \text{N}$. Find the amplitude at low $\omega$, at resonance, and at high $\omega$. (Direct application of 1.)

  1. $\omega_0 = 10$, $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma) = 5$.

  2. Low $\omega$: $A \approx F_0/k = 0.01\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{cm}$.

  3. At $\omega = \omega_0$: $A = Q\cdot F_0/k = 5\ \text{cm}$.

  4. At $\omega = 100$: $A \approx F_0/(m\omega^2) = 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.1\ \text{mm}$.

Check

Resonance is 5× the static deflection — the quality factor in action. Far above $\omega_0$ the mass barely moves at all ✓.

Example

Explain why pushing a swing at the right rhythm makes it go higher but random pushing does not. (Qualitative; based on 1.)

  1. A swing is a weakly damped oscillator with natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$. For a child on a swing, $L \approx 2\ \text{m}$, $\omega_0 \approx 2.2\ \text{rad/s}$, period about $2.8\ \text{s}$.

  2. Push at $\omega = \omega_0$ (every $\sim 2.8\ \text{s}$): steady-state amplitude = $Q \times$ (single-push displacement). The swing can get very high even with modest pushes.

  3. Push randomly: pushes partly cancel each other; no coherent buildup.

Check

Pushing at the swing's natural tempo exploits the resonance amplification factor $Q$. ✓

Example

A swing bridge with $Q = 20$ and natural frequency $f_0 = 2\ \text{Hz}$ is excited by soldiers marching at $2\ \text{Hz}$. How much is the sway amplified? (Qualitative, based on 1 resonance result.)

  1. Amplification at resonance is the factor $Q = 20$.

  2. So the same force that would cause a $1\ \text{cm}$ static deflection now produces a $20\ \text{cm}$ sway.

Check

Historical reason soldiers "break step" when crossing bridges. Each tiny periodic push adds up resonantly ✓.
