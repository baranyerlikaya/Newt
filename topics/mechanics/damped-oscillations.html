Damped Oscillations

Adding a drag force −bẋ gives ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = 0, with three regimes depending on γ/ω₀: underdamped (oscillates while decaying), critically damped and overdamped.

In short

Add friction and the oscillation dies out. How it dies depends on how strong the damping is compared to the natural frequency $\omega_0$ 1.

Use the ratio $\gamma = b/(2m)$ versus $\omega_0$:

  • Weak damping ($\gamma < \omega_0$): "underdamped". Oscillates while shrinking. A guitar string, a child's swing, a tuning fork.
  • Just-right damping ($\gamma = \omega_0$): "critically damped". No oscillation, returns to rest as fast as possible. Shock absorbers, door closers.
  • Heavy damping ($\gamma > \omega_0$): "overdamped". Also no oscillation, but slower than critical. Slamming a car door with hydraulic damping.

Quality factor $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$ measures how good the oscillator is at keeping going 1:

  • A violin string: $Q \sim 10^3$, so it rings for about $300$ cycles.
  • A car suspension: $Q \approx 1$, bounces barely once.
  • Quartz crystal in a watch: $Q \sim 10^5$.

Per cycle, the fraction of energy lost is $\Delta E/E \approx 2\pi/Q$: high $Q$ = loses little per cycle = rings long 1.

Theory

The equation

Add a damping force $F_d = -b\dot x$ (fluid drag at low speeds) to Hooke's law: $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$. Rearranging with $\gamma = b/(2m)$ and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ 1 §Damped Oscillations:

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0.$$

Try $x = e^{\lambda t}$: the characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 2\gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0$, with roots

$$\lambda_{1,2} = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}.$$

Three cases depending on the sign of the discriminant 1.

Underdamped (γ < ω₀)

Complex roots $\lambda = -\gamma \pm i\omega_1$ where $\omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$ 1 §Underdamped Oscillations:

$$x(t) = R\,e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi).$$

A cosine inside an exponentially decaying envelope. The damped angular frequency $\omega_1$ is slightly lower than $\omega_0$ — more damping means slower oscillations 1.

After one period $T = 2\pi/\omega_1$ the amplitude has shrunk by a factor $e^{-\gamma T}$. The energy drops by $e^{-2\gamma T}$ per period: $E(t+T) = E(t)\,e^{-2\gamma T}$ 1.

Critically damped (γ = ω₀)

Repeated real root $\lambda = -\gamma$. The general solution is 1 §Critically Damped Oscillations:

$$x(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-\gamma t}.$$

No oscillation, and the return to equilibrium is the fastest smooth decay — exactly the right damping for shock absorbers and door closers 1.

Overdamped (γ > ω₀)

Two distinct real negative roots $\lambda_{1,2} = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}$. Both exponentials decay 1 §Overdamped Motion:

$$x(t) = A\,e^{\lambda_1 t} + B\,e^{\lambda_2 t}.$$

The mass crawls back to equilibrium without oscillating. Heavy damping makes the approach slower than critical 1.

Quality factor

The quality factor $Q$ measures how long the oscillations last 1 §Quality Factor:

$$Q = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma}.$$

For weak damping ($\gamma \ll \omega_0$), the fractional energy lost per cycle is $\Delta E/E \approx 2\pi/Q$. A violin string has $Q \sim 10^3$, a car shock absorber $Q \sim 1$, a quartz crystal $Q \sim 10^5$ 1.

The amplitude drops to $1/e$ in $t_{1/e} = 1/\gamma$. The number of cycles in this time is $N \approx Q/\pi$: a high-$Q$ oscillator rings many times before fading 1.

Note

$\omega_0$ is the natural frequency of the undamped system. The actual oscillation when there is damping is at $\omega_1 < \omega_0$. For high-$Q$ systems $\omega_1 \approx \omega_0(1 - 1/(8Q^2))$ — the correction is tiny 1.

Warning

Critical damping gives the fastest return to rest, not the fastest return to the origin. If you want to zero a measuring instrument quickly without overshooting, you want critical damping; if you want to damp an oscillation out as quickly as possible, slightly underdamped can be faster at first but overshoots 1.

Visualization

A mass on a spring with damping $b$. Keep $k = 12$, $m = 1$, so $\omega_0 \approx 3.46$ and critical damping is at $b = 2\sqrt{km} \approx 6.9$. $b = 0$: no damping, oscillates forever. $b = 0.3$ (default): weak damping, oscillates while the envelope shrinks exponentially. $b = 7$: critically damped, returns to rest quickly without oscillating. $b = 20$: overdamped, crawls back more slowly.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A $1\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring $k = 100\ \text{N/m}$ has damping $b = 2\ \text{kg/s}$. Find $\gamma$, $\omega_0$, which regime, $\omega_1$, $Q$ and $t_{1/e}$. (Direct application of 1.)

  1. $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = 10\ \text{rad/s}$. $\gamma = b/(2m) = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

  2. $\gamma < \omega_0$: underdamped. $\omega_1 = \sqrt{100 - 1} \approx 9.95\ \text{rad/s}$ — very close to $\omega_0$.

  3. $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma) = 5$. $t_{1/e} = 1/\gamma = 1\ \text{s}$.

Check

Cycles before amplitude drops to $1/e$: $N \approx Q/\pi \approx 1.6$ — about 1–2 oscillations before noticeable decay ✓.

Example

For what damping $b$ is a mass-spring system critically damped? Use $m$ and $k$. (From 1, §Critically Damped Oscillations.)

  1. Critical: $\gamma = \omega_0$, i.e. $b/(2m) = \sqrt{k/m}$.

  2. Solve: $b_{\text{crit}} = 2\sqrt{km}$.

Check

Dimensionally $\sqrt{kg \cdot N/m} = \sqrt{kg^2/s^2} = kg/s$ ✓, correct units for a drag coefficient.

Example

A guitar string rings for $N$ cycles before dropping to $1/e$. Estimate its $Q$ and the time the note lasts if $f = 440\ \text{Hz}$. (Application of 1, §Quality Factor.)

  1. $Q \approx \pi N$. For $N = 300$ cycles: $Q \approx 942$.

  2. $t_{1/e} = Q/(\pi f) \approx 942/(\pi \times 440) \approx 0.68\ \text{s}$.

Check

Matches a plucked steel string that rings noticeably for about a second before fading. ✓
