Newton's 2nd Law in Rotational Form

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, Newton's second law becomes τ = Iα, the rotational analogue of F = ma.

In short

Newton's second law, rotational version. For a rigid body spinning about a fixed axis 1; 2:

$$\tau = I\alpha.$$

Same shape as $F = ma$. Substitute torque for force, moment of inertia for mass, angular acceleration for linear acceleration.

Where does it come from? From $dL/dt = \tau$: since $L = I\omega$ and $I$ is constant for a rigid body, $dL/dt = I\,d\omega/dt = I\alpha$ 1; 2.

How to use it, step by step:

  1. Pick the axis.
  2. Find each force and its torque about that axis: moment arm × force.
  3. Add torques to get net $\tau$.
  4. Find $I$ about that axis.
  5. $\alpha = \tau/I$.

Like with linear motion, if $\tau$ is constant, $\alpha$ is constant, and the angle-velocity-time formulas look exactly like $v = v_0 + at$ and $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2$, with $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$.

Only external torques count — internal ones cancel in pairs, just like internal forces 2.

Theory

From dL/dt = τ to τ = Iα

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, $L = I\omega$ with $I$ the moment of inertia about that axis (see Topic 4.3). Since $I$ is constant for a rigid body 1 §Angular-Momentum Form of the 2nd Axiom; 2 §17.4.1,

$$\frac{dL}{dt} = I\,\frac{d\omega}{dt} = I\alpha.$$

The rotational second law $dL/dt = \tau$ becomes

$$\boxed{\tau = I\alpha,}$$

where $\tau$ is the component of the net external torque along the rotation axis 2 §17.4.1.

The parallel between translation and rotation

Line up the two laws 1:

  • Translation: $\mathbf F = m\mathbf a$
  • Rotation (fixed axis): $\tau = I\alpha$

Replace: force → torque, mass → moment of inertia, acceleration → angular acceleration. The same equation of motion, in rotational language 1 §Angular-Momentum Form of the 2nd Axiom; 2 §17.4.1.

Internal torques cancel

Like internal forces, internal torques come in Newton's-third-law pairs along the line joining particles. Their torques about any point cancel in pairs (since $\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j$ is parallel to $\mathbf F_{j,i}$) 2 §17.4.1:

$$\sum_i(\boldsymbol\tau^{\text{int}}_i) = 0.$$

So only the external torques matter in $\tau = I\alpha$ 2 §17.4.1.

Solving a rotational problem

The recipe is the mirror of the translational one 2 §17.4:

  1. Choose an axis of rotation.
  2. Draw the forces and compute their torques about that axis: $\tau_i = r_{\perp,i}F_i$ (positive if it tends to rotate the chosen positive way).
  3. Compute $I$ about that axis.
  4. Apply $\sum\tau_i = I\alpha$ to get $\alpha$.
  5. Integrate to get $\omega(t)$ and $\theta(t)$.

Constant torque gives constant $\alpha$, so $\omega(t) = \omega_0 + \alpha t$ and $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$ 2 §17.4.1.

Note

For an axis through the centre of mass, external forces acting at the centre (such as gravity on a symmetric body) produce no torque about that axis. That is why hanging a body from its centre of mass makes it rotate only when an off-axis torque is applied 2 §17.4.2.

Warning

$\tau = I\alpha$ applies about a fixed axis (or the centre-of-mass axis of a rigid body). For a more general axis, both $\mathbf L$ and $\boldsymbol\tau$ must be treated as vectors, and $I$ becomes a tensor — outside the scope of this topic 2 §17.4.

Visualization

A disc with torque $\tau$ applied about its axis. The angular acceleration is $\alpha = \tau/I$ (shown live). The disc speeds up: $\omega = \alpha t$ grows linearly, and $\theta = \tfrac12\alpha t^2$ grows as $t^2$. The graph at the bottom shows $\omega(t)$: a straight line with slope $\alpha$. Try doubling $\tau$: the line is twice as steep. Double $I$ instead: half as steep.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A uniform disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ (with $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$) is free to rotate about its centre. A tangential force $F$ is applied at the rim. Find the angular acceleration and the time to reach angular velocity $\omega$ from rest. (Direct application of 2, §17.4.1.)

  1. Torque: $\tau = RF$, along the axis.

  2. $\alpha = \tau/I = RF/(\tfrac12 MR^2) = 2F/(MR)$.

  3. From rest: $\omega = \alpha t$, so $t = \omega/\alpha = MR\omega/(2F)$.

Check

Bigger $F$ or smaller $M$: faster spin-up ✓. The factor of 2 comes from the disc's $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$, half as much as a hoop of the same mass and radius.

Example

A pulley of moment of inertia $I$ and radius $R$ has a string wrapped around it; a block of mass $m$ hangs from the string and is released. Find the block's downward acceleration, assuming the string doesn't slip. (From 2, Example 16.4, adapted.)

  1. Block: $ma = mg - T$.

  2. Pulley: tangential tension $T$ at radius $R$ gives $\tau = RT$, so $I\alpha = RT$.

  3. No-slip: $a = R\alpha$, so $T = I\alpha/R = Ia/R^2$.

  4. Substitute: $ma = mg - Ia/R^2$, so $a = \dfrac{mg}{m + I/R^2}$.

Check

Massless pulley ($I = 0$): $a = g$ ✓. Very massive pulley ($I/R^2 \gg m$): $a \to 0$ ✓.

Example

A thin rod of mass $M$ and length $L$ is pivoted at one end and released from horizontal. Find its angular acceleration at the moment of release. (From 2, §17.4.2 and Example 17.9.)

  1. Moment of inertia about the pivot (end of rod): $I_{\text{end}} = \tfrac13 ML^2$.

  2. Gravity acts at the centre of mass, distance $L/2$ from the pivot, perpendicular to the rod when horizontal: $\tau = (L/2)(Mg) = MgL/2$.

  3. $\alpha = \tau/I = (MgL/2)/(\tfrac13 ML^2) = 3g/(2L)$.

Check

Units: $g/L$ has units $\text{s}^{-2}$ ✓. For $L = 1\ \text{m}$, $\alpha \approx 14.7\ \text{rad/s}^2$: the tip's tangential acceleration is $L\alpha = 1.5g > g$, so a coin on the tip falls slower than the tip itself.
