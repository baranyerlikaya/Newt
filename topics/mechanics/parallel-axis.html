Moment of Inertia: Shapes and the Parallel-Axis Theorem

Moments of inertia of common shapes about their CM, and the parallel-axis theorem that gets you the moment about any other parallel axis.

In short

Moment of inertia is "mass for rotation": how hard it is to twist a body (Topic 4.3). It depends on both the mass and how the mass is spread.

Standard shapes (about an axis through the centre of mass) 1:

  • Thin rod, axis through middle: $\tfrac{1}{12}ML^2$.
  • Thin ring: $MR^2$ (all mass at the rim).
  • Uniform disc: $\tfrac12 MR^2$.
  • Solid sphere: $\tfrac25 MR^2$ (mass is centrally concentrated).
  • Hollow sphere: $\tfrac23 MR^2$ (mass on the surface).

Hollow shapes have bigger $I$ than solid ones, because their mass sits further from the axis.

Parallel-axis theorem 1: if you move the axis sideways by a distance $d$ (parallel to the original), the new moment of inertia is:

$$I = I_{\text{CM}} + Md^2.$$

Any parallel-shifted axis has a larger $I$ than the CM axis.

Example: a rod, about one end instead of the centre. $I_{\text{end}} = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac13 ML^2$. Four times bigger.

Rolling race: solid cylinder beats hollow cylinder down a ramp because its smaller $I$ means less energy spent spinning, more left for forward motion. Try it with a tin can and a solid bottle.

Theory

Moment of inertia for a continuous body

For a continuous body about a chosen axis, replace the sum $\sum m_ir_i^2$ with an integral 1 §16.3.1:

$$I = \int r_\perp^2\,dm,$$

where $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the mass element $dm$. For a body of uniform density $\rho$: $dm = \rho\,dV$ and $I = \rho\int r_\perp^2\,dV$.

Standard shapes

Results for uniform bodies, axis through the CM 1 §16.3.2, Example 16.2:

  • Thin rod of length $L$, perpendicular axis through centre: $I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2$.
  • Thin rod of length $L$, perpendicular axis through end: $I = \tfrac{1}{3}ML^2$.
  • Thin ring of radius $R$, axis through centre perpendicular to the ring: $I = MR^2$.
  • Uniform disc of radius $R$, axis through centre perpendicular to disc: $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$.
  • Solid sphere of radius $R$, axis through centre: $I = \tfrac25 MR^2$.
  • Hollow sphere (thin shell) of radius $R$: $I = \tfrac23 MR^2$.
  • Solid cylinder (radius $R$, length $L$), axis along symmetry: $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$.

Hollow shapes have more mass far from the axis, so they have bigger $I$ than solid ones of the same mass and radius — and roll down ramps slower.

Parallel-axis theorem

If $I_{\text{CM}}$ is the moment of inertia about an axis through the CM, then about any parallel axis at perpendicular distance $d$ 1 §16.3.3:

$$\boxed{I = I_{\text{CM}} + Md^2.}$$

Shift the axis out by $d$ and add $Md^2$. Equivalent: the CM axis always gives the smallest moment of inertia among all parallel axes 1.

Example: thin rod, axis through an end: $I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + \tfrac14 ML^2 = \tfrac13 ML^2$. Matches the direct result above ✓.

Deriving a moment of inertia (rod example)

Thin rod of mass $M$, length $L$, linear density $\lambda = M/L$. Axis perpendicular through the centre. Mass element at $x$: $dm = \lambda\,dx$. Distance to axis: $|x|$. Then 1 §16.3.2

$$I = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\lambda\,x^2\,dx = \lambda\,\frac{L^3}{12} = \frac{ML^2}{12}.$$

Same method works for any 1D body: a quick integral along the length.

Note

Moments of inertia scale with $MR^2$ (or $ML^2$ for rods). For geometrically similar shapes, the dimensionless prefactor (e.g. $\tfrac12$ for a disc, $\tfrac25$ for a sphere) depends only on the mass distribution, not on the overall size 1.

Warning

Parallel-axis theorem needs an axis parallel to the CM axis. For an axis through a different point and in a different direction, use the full tensor relation or integrate directly 1 §16.3.3.

Visualization

Four point masses on a cross at adjustable radii $r_1$ and $r_2$, showing $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ in action. Pull the masses in → $I$ drops rapidly (as $r^2$). Spread them out → $I$ grows. A real extended body is just infinitely many such masses integrated together.

Worked examples

Example

Find the moment of inertia of a uniform disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about its symmetry axis, by integration. (From 1, Example 16.2.)

  1. Surface density $\sigma = M/(\pi R^2)$. Ring at radius $r$ with width $dr$: $dm = \sigma\,2\pi r\,dr$.

  2. Its distance from the axis is $r$: $dI = r^2\,dm = 2\pi\sigma\,r^3\,dr$.

  3. Integrate: $I = \int_0^R 2\pi\sigma\,r^3\,dr = 2\pi\sigma\,R^4/4 = \tfrac12 MR^2$.

Check

Same as the table entry ✓. A thin ring at radius $R$ would give $I = MR^2$ — twice as large, because all the mass is at the outer rim.

Example

Find the moment of inertia of a solid disc about an axis tangent to its rim, parallel to its symmetry axis. (Application of the parallel-axis theorem.)

  1. CM moment about the symmetry axis: $I_{\text{CM}} = \tfrac12 MR^2$.

  2. Parallel axis at the rim, distance $d = R$: $I = \tfrac12 MR^2 + MR^2 = \tfrac32 MR^2$.

Check

Three times larger than for the central axis: moving the axis out to $R$ adds $MR^2$ ✓.

Example

Why does a hollow cylinder roll down a ramp slower than a solid one of the same mass and radius? (Qualitative, using Topic 11.3 and the moments above.)

  1. Rolling KE: $K = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12 MV^2(1 + I/(MR^2))$ (since $V = \omega R$).

  2. Solid cylinder: $I/(MR^2) = \tfrac12$. Hollow cylinder (thin shell): $I/(MR^2) = 1$.

  3. Hollow one needs more energy per unit $V^2$ — so for the same height drop $Mgh$, it ends up with lower $V$. Slower.

Check

Famous "which cylinder wins the race" demo: solid beats hollow every time, regardless of mass and radius ✓.
