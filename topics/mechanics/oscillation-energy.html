Energy of Simple Oscillations

For simple harmonic motion the total energy is constant, equal to ½kR², and it trades back and forth between kinetic and potential as the oscillator swings.

In short

A spring oscillator never loses energy (no friction yet). But kinetic and potential take turns 1:

  • At the ends of the swing ($x = \pm R$): zero speed, all energy stored in the spring as $U$.
  • At the middle ($x = 0$): spring at natural length, all energy as motion $K$.
  • In between: some $K$, some $U$, but the total is always the same.

The constant total energy is 1:

$$E = \tfrac12 kR^2.$$

So the amplitude $R$ is set by how much energy you gave it. Give a swing a bigger push → wider swing. Interesting bit: the period doesn't change — small swings and big swings both take the same time (the beat depends on $m$ and $k$ only).

Maximum speed: at $x = 0$, $v_{\max} = R\omega_0$.

On average over a full cycle, exactly half the energy is kinetic and half is potential 1. That's a surprisingly deep fact — it generalizes to many parabolic wells, as we saw in Topic 5.5.

Theory

Total mechanical energy

For a mass on a spring, $K = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2$ and $U = \tfrac12 k x^2$. With the SHM solution $x(t) = R\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)$ 1 §Energy of Simple Oscillations:

$$\begin{gathered}K(t) = \tfrac12 mR^2\omega_0^2\sin^2(\omega_0 t+\phi) \\ U(t) = \tfrac12 kR^2\cos^2(\omega_0 t+\phi).\end{gathered}$$

Adding and using $\sin^2+\cos^2 = 1$ and $k = m\omega_0^2$ 1:

$$\boxed{E = K + U = \tfrac12 kR^2 = \tfrac12 mR^2\omega_0^2 = \text{const.}}$$

Energy traded, total conserved

$K$ and $U$ each oscillate at twice the oscillation frequency, $2\omega_0$. When one is at a maximum the other is at a minimum; their sum is always $E$ 1. At the turning points $x = \pm R$, $K = 0$ and all energy is potential; at the centre $x = 0$, $U = 0$ and all energy is kinetic. The maximum speed is

$$v_{\max} = R\omega_0,$$

and the maximum $U$ is $\tfrac12 kR^2 = E$.

Amplitude from energy

Rearranging $E = \tfrac12 kR^2$:

$$R = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}.$$

So the amplitude is set by the energy you gave the oscillator — not by the spring constant alone. Give it more energy, it swings further 1.

The speed at any point $x$ follows from $K = E - U$:

$$v(x) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}\left(E - \tfrac12 kx^2\right)} = \pm\omega_0\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}.$$

Averages

Since $\overline{\sin^2(\omega_0 t+\phi)} = \overline{\cos^2(\omega_0 t+\phi)} = \tfrac12$, the time-averaged energies are 1:

$$\langle K\rangle = \langle U\rangle = \tfrac12 E = \tfrac14 kR^2.$$

The oscillator spends on average half its energy as kinetic and half as potential — a general property of quadratic potentials (virial theorem in miniature).

Note

Any potential $U(x)$ that has a stable minimum behaves like $\tfrac12 k_{\text{eff}}(x-x_0)^2$ near the bottom, with $k_{\text{eff}} = U''(x_0)$ — so the same energy formulas apply 1.

Warning

The energy conservation $E = \tfrac12 kR^2$ is exact only for a frictionless (undamped) oscillator. Any drag or friction drains mechanical energy into heat; see Topic 6.3 on damped oscillations 1.

Visualization

A pendulum swinging with no damping. The bars on the right show $K$, $U$ and the total $E$. $K$ and $U$ trade back and forth, but $E$ stays flat. At the lowest point, all energy is kinetic; at the highest point, all potential. If you add damping, the total $E$ shrinks — the mechanical energy is leaking into heat, breaking conservation.

Worked examples

Example

A $1\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 100\ \text{N/m}$ has amplitude $5\ \text{cm}$. Find the energy, max speed, and the speed at $x = 2.5\ \text{cm}$. (Direct application of 1.)

  1. $\omega_0 = \sqrt{100/1} = 10\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. Total energy: $E = \tfrac12 kR^2 = \tfrac12(100)(0.05)^2 = 0.125\ \text{J}$.

  3. Max speed: $v_{\max} = R\omega_0 = 0.05\times 10 = 0.5\ \text{m/s}$.

  4. At $x = 0.025$: $v = \omega_0\sqrt{R^2 - x^2} = 10\sqrt{0.0025 - 0.000625} = 10\sqrt{0.001875} \approx 0.433\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

Check with energy: $K = E - U = 0.125 - \tfrac12(100)(0.025)^2 = 0.125 - 0.03125 = 0.09375\ \text{J}$, so $v = \sqrt{2K/m} \approx 0.433\ \text{m/s}$ ✓.

Example

A spring is given twice the energy. What happens to the amplitude, period, and maximum speed? (Application of $E = \tfrac12 kR^2$ and $v_{\max} = R\omega_0$.)

  1. $R = \sqrt{2E/k}$: doubling $E$ multiplies $R$ by $\sqrt 2$.

  2. Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ — unchanged.

  3. $v_{\max} = R\omega_0$: multiplied by $\sqrt 2$.

Check

The amplitude isn't proportional to $E$; both $R^2 \propto E$ and $v_{\max}^2 \propto E$. ✓

Example

A pendulum of length $L$, amplitude $\theta_0$ (small), bob mass $m$. Find the oscillation energy. (From 1, Worked Examples and §Energy of Simple Oscillations.)

  1. The effective stiffness from the small-angle equation $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\theta = 0$ is $k_{\text{eff}} = mg/L$, with "coordinate" $\theta$. Then $E = \tfrac12 k_{\text{eff}}R^2 = \tfrac12 (mg/L)\theta_0^2 \cdot L^2 = \tfrac12 mgL\theta_0^2$.

  2. Alternatively, the maximum height above the bottom is $L(1-\cos\theta_0) \approx \tfrac12 L\theta_0^2$ for small $\theta_0$, so $E \approx mgL\theta_0^2/2$ — same answer.

Check

The max speed is at the bottom: $v_{\max} = \sqrt{2E/m} = \theta_0\sqrt{gL}$, matching the direct $L\theta_0\omega_0$ ✓.
