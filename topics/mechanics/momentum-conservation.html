Conservation of Linear and Angular Momentum

For an isolated system, the total linear momentum is constant; if no external torque acts, so is the total angular momentum. Both come directly from Newton's laws.

In short

Big idea: if nothing from outside pushes or twists, the total "amount of motion" stays the same.

Linear momentum $\mathbf p = m\mathbf v$ (see 2.5, 3.2): when no outside force acts, the total $\mathbf p$ of the system never changes 1; 2.

Gun recoil, boat jumping, rocket thrust, Newton's cradle — all the same rule:

$$\sum m_i\mathbf v_{i,\text{before}} = \sum m_i\mathbf v_{i,\text{after}}.$$

Internal forces (A pushes B, B pushes A) cancel by Newton's third law, so they can't change the total 2.

Angular momentum $\mathbf L = \sum \mathbf r_i\times\mathbf p_i$ (see 4.2): when no outside torque acts, total $\mathbf L$ about the chosen point stays the same 1; 3.

Figure skater: arms in → smaller $I$ → faster spin ($I\omega$ constant). Diver tucking into a flip. A cat landing on its feet 1.

Work with components. If gravity pulls down but no horizontal force acts, horizontal $p$ is conserved while vertical $p$ isn't. That's projectile motion 1.

Watch out: $p$ and $L$ are different conservation laws under different conditions. A spinning top on a smooth table conserves $L$ (no torque about its axis) but hardly conserves anything about $p$.

Theory

Conservation of linear momentum

For a system of particles, the total momentum is $\mathbf p = \sum_i m_i\mathbf v_i$. By Newton's second law for the system, only the external forces change $\mathbf p$: internal forces cancel in third-law pairs 1 §Conservation of Linear Momentum; 2 §10.7. Hence

$$\dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf F_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf p = \text{const.}$$

An isolated system (no external force) conserves its total momentum. A collision is the classic case: during the brief contact, internal forces between the bodies can be huge, but external forces act long enough to be negligible 2 §10.7.

Component-wise conservation

Conservation is really a vector statement. If the external force vanishes only in one direction, the corresponding component of momentum is still conserved 1:

$$p_s = \mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf s}, \qquad \dot p_s = \mathbf F_{\text{ext}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf s} = F_{\text{ext},s}.$$

Projectile example: horizontal momentum is conserved (no horizontal force), vertical momentum is not (gravity).

Conservation of angular momentum

Angular momentum of a system about a point $O$ is $\mathbf L = \sum_i\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf p_i$. By the rotational second law, only external torques change it 1 §Conservation of Angular Momentum; 3 §19.4:

$$\dot{\mathbf L} = \boldsymbol\tau_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf L = \text{const.}$$

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, $L = I\omega$, so 3 §19.8:

$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2.$$

Change the body's shape (change $I$), and $\omega$ changes to compensate. That is why a figure skater spins faster by pulling in her arms (Topic 4.3 Example 3).

Collisions

During a short collision, external impulses are negligible, so total momentum is conserved 1 Worked Example “Inelastic collision of two hockey pucks”; 2 §10.7:

$$m_1\mathbf v_{1i} + m_2\mathbf v_{2i} = m_1\mathbf v_{1f} + m_2\mathbf v_{2f}.$$

Kinetic energy may or may not be conserved: elastic collisions conserve it; inelastic do not; perfectly inelastic collisions (bodies stick together) lose the most 1.

Centre of mass

Equivalently, in an isolated system the centre of mass $\mathbf R = \sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i/M$ moves with constant velocity, because $M\dot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf p$ 2 §10.5. Internal forces can do anything inside the system; they cannot change $\mathbf R$'s motion.

Note

Momentum and angular momentum are conserved separately, under separate conditions ($\mathbf F_{\text{ext}} = 0$ vs $\boldsymbol\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$). A system can conserve one without the other: a spinning skater on a frictionless pivot has fixed $\mathbf L$ but no particular conservation of $\mathbf p$ 1; 3 §19.4.

Warning

Angular momentum is always measured about a specific point. Switching reference points gives a different $\mathbf L$, and may give a different conservation statement (because the torque about a different point is different) 3 §19.2.

Visualization

Two carts: cart 1 (dark) rolls at $v_1$ into cart 2 (grey) at rest, and they stick (a perfectly inelastic collision). The bars show $p_1$, $p_2$ and their sum $p_1 + p_2$. The total bar never changes — momentum is conserved. After the collision both move at $v_f = m_1v_1/(m_1+m_2)$. Change the masses: the final speed drops as $m_2$ grows. Kinetic energy, unlike momentum, is not conserved: see Topic 5.2.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A $5\ \text{kg}$ rifle fires a $10\ \text{g}$ bullet at $300\ \text{m/s}$. Find the recoil speed of the rifle. (Standard application of 1, §Conservation of Linear Momentum.)

  1. Before firing, both are at rest: total momentum is zero.

  2. No net external horizontal force on the (rifle + bullet) during firing, so total $p$ stays zero: $m_r v_r + m_b v_b = 0$.

  3. $v_r = -\dfrac{m_b v_b}{m_r} = -\dfrac{0.010 \times 300}{5} = -0.6\ \text{m/s}$, backwards.

Check

Mass ratio 500:1 gives speed ratio 500:1 in the opposite direction ✓.

Example

A ball of mass $m$ moving at $v_0$ hits a resting ball of the same mass head-on in an elastic collision. Find the velocities after. (Standard result; method from 2, §10.7, applied to equal masses.)

  1. Momentum: $mv_0 = mv_1 + mv_2$, so $v_0 = v_1 + v_2$.

  2. Energy: $\tfrac12 mv_0^2 = \tfrac12 mv_1^2 + \tfrac12 mv_2^2$, so $v_0^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2$.

  3. Combined: $(v_1 + v_2)^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2 \Rightarrow 2v_1v_2 = 0$. So either $v_1 = 0$ (and $v_2 = v_0$) or the trivial $v_2 = 0$.

  4. The physical answer: the incoming ball stops and the hit ball moves off at $v_0$.

Check

That's the classic Newton's-cradle outcome ✓. For unequal masses, use the general elastic-collision formulas.

Example

A skater has $I_1 = 5\ \text{kg·m}^2$ arms out, spinning at $2\ \text{rev/s}$. She pulls her arms in so that $I_2 = 2\ \text{kg·m}^2$. Find her new angular velocity and the change in rotational kinetic energy. (From Topic 4.3 Example 3, based on 1, Worked Example “Conservation of angular momentum”.)

  1. No external torque: $L = I\omega$ is conserved.

  2. $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2 \Rightarrow \omega_2 = (I_1/I_2)\omega_1 = 2.5 \times 2 = 5\ \text{rev/s}$.

  3. $K_2/K_1 = (I_2\omega_2^2)/(I_1\omega_1^2) = (I_1/I_2) = 2.5$: energy grows by a factor of $2.5$, coming from the skater's muscle work 1.

Check

$L_1 = 5\times 2 = 10$, $L_2 = 2\times 5 = 10$: same ✓. $K$ is not conserved even though $L$ is.

Example

A boy of mass $40\ \text{kg}$ jumps off a $100\ \text{kg}$ boat at rest, pushing off with a velocity of $2\ \text{m/s}$ relative to the water. Find the boat's velocity after. (Direct application of 2, §10.7.)

  1. Before: everything at rest, total $p = 0$.

  2. After: $40 \times 2 + 100 \times v_{\text{boat}} = 0$.

  3. $v_{\text{boat}} = -0.8\ \text{m/s}$: the boat drifts backwards at $0.8\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

Centre of mass of (boy + boat) stays at rest, as required for an isolated system ✓.
