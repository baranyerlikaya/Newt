Conservation of Total Energy, Energy as Equation of Motion

For a particle under conservative forces (with time-independent potential), the total mechanical energy K + U is constant. This gives a first-order equation of motion.

In short

Total energy = motion energy + stored energy 1; 2:

$$E = K + U = \tfrac12 mv^2 + U.$$

The big rule: if the only forces are conservative (and $U$ doesn't change with time), $E$ is constant. You can swap $K$ for $U$ and back, but the total never changes 1; 2.

Examples:

  • Dropping a ball: $U = mgh$ drops to zero, $K$ grows to $mgh$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$.
  • Pendulum: at the top, all $U$; at the bottom, all $K$; back and forth.
  • Spring: at the ends of the swing ($x = \pm A$), all $U = \tfrac12 kA^2$; at the middle, all $K$.

Where can the particle go? Only where $E \ge U(x)$ (otherwise $K$ would be negative, which is impossible). Places where $E = U$ are turning points: there the particle stops for an instant and reverses 1.

Energy is a shortcut. Instead of solving $F = ma$ (a second-order equation), you can often use $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + U(x) = \text{const}$ to get the speed at any point without knowing the whole motion 1.

When it fails: friction and drag don't have a potential energy. They turn mechanical energy into heat, so $E$ drops. The total energy (including heat) is still conserved — that's thermodynamics 2.

Theory

Conservation of mechanical energy

The mechanical energy of a particle is the sum of kinetic and potential energies 1 §Conservation of Mechanical Energy; 2 §14.5:

$$E = K + U = \tfrac12 mv^2 + U(\mathbf r).$$

Differentiate with respect to time, using $dK/dt = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$ (work-energy theorem rate form) and $dU/dt = \nabla U\cdot\mathbf v + \partial U/\partial t$ 1:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = (\mathbf F + \nabla U)\cdot\mathbf v + \frac{\partial U}{\partial t}.$$

For a conservative force $\mathbf F = -\nabla U$, the first term vanishes. If $U$ is also time-independent, $\partial U/\partial t = 0$, and 1

$$\boxed{\frac{dE}{dt} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; E = K + U = \text{const.}}$$

Allowed and forbidden regions

Since $K \ge 0$ always, conservation implies 1 §More on the Potential Energy

$$E \ge U(x).$$

Regions where $U(x) > E$ are classically forbidden: the particle can never enter them (it would need $K < 0$). The boundaries where $U(x) = E$ are turning points: there $K = 0$, the particle stops instantaneously, and if the force there is nonzero it reverses 1.

Energy as a first integral of motion

In one dimension, solve $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + U(x)$ for the speed 1 §Mechanical Energy as a First Integral of Motion:

$$v = \frac{dx}{dt} = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{m}\bigl[E - U(x)\bigr]}.$$

This is a first-order equation — one integration below the second-order Newton equation. Separate and integrate 1:

$$t - t_0 = \int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{dx'}{\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{m}[E - U(x')]}}.$$

If the integral can be done, it gives $x(t)$ directly from $E$ and $U(x)$, without ever solving $m\ddot x = -dU/dx$ 1.

In 2D or 3D, $E = \text{const}$ fixes the speed but not the direction, so it isn't enough on its own; you need other conserved quantities too (such as $L$) 1.

When energy isn't conserved

Mechanical energy is not conserved if 1; 2 §14.7:

  • a non-conservative force acts (friction, drag): it does path-dependent work that cannot be captured by any $U$; the energy lost goes into heat;
  • a time-dependent potential is present (an external agent pumps energy in or out).

In those cases the right statement is energy balance: $\Delta E_{\text{mechanical}} = W_{\text{non-conservative}}$ 2 §14.7.1.

From Newton to energy and back

The conservation of energy is just Newton's second law, integrated once. For a particle under a constant force $F$, you can derive $x(t)$ either from $m\ddot x = F$ (two integrations) or from $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 - Fx$ (one separation and square) — same answer 1 Worked Example “Using Total Energy as the Equation of Motion for a Particle Under a Constant Force”.

Note

Energy conservation is often easier to apply than $F = ma$ because it is a scalar equation and uses only the start and end states, skipping the details of the path 1 Worked Example “Velocity with respect to distance”.

Warning

Potential energy is defined only for conservative forces. Don't write "$U$ of friction": friction's work depends on the path, so it has no potential energy 2 §14.2.

Visualization

A pendulum swinging (optionally with damping). The three bars on the right show kinetic energy $K$, potential energy $U = mgL(1-\cos\theta)$, and the total $E = K + U$. With damping 0: $E$ stays constant and $K, U$ trade back and forth. Add damping: the total $E$ drops — the "lost" energy becomes heat, which is where the mechanical-energy conservation breaks.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A ball of mass $m$ is dropped from height $h$. Find its speed when it hits the ground using energy conservation. (Direct application of 1; 2 §14.5.1.)

  1. Take $U = mgy$ with $U(0) = 0$. Initial: $K_i = 0$, $U_i = mgh$, so $E = mgh$.

  2. Final: $U_f = 0$, so $K_f = E = mgh$.

  3. $\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgh \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2gh}$.

Check

Same result as from $m\ddot y = -mg$ with $v = 0$ at $y = h$ (Topic 3.3), and it doesn't depend on $m$ ✓.

Example

A block slides down a frictionless incline of angle $\theta$, starting from rest. Find its speed after moving a distance $d$ along the slope. (From 1, Worked Example “Velocity with respect to distance”, Solution 2.)

  1. Drop in height: $\Delta y = -d\sin\theta$, so $\Delta U = -mgd\sin\theta$.

  2. Conservation: $K_f - K_i = -\Delta U = mgd\sin\theta$, so $\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgd\sin\theta$.

  3. $v = \sqrt{2gd\sin\theta}$.

Check

Height dropped $h = d\sin\theta$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ — the same as free fall from height $h$. Expected: no friction, so all $U$ turns into $K$ ✓.

Example

A mass on a spring of constant $k$ oscillates with amplitude $A$. Find the maximum speed. (Standard application; derivation from 2, §14.4.2.)

  1. $E = \tfrac12 kx^2 + \tfrac12 mv^2$. At the turning points ($x = \pm A$), $v = 0$, so $E = \tfrac12 kA^2$.

  2. At the centre ($x = 0$), $U = 0$, so all energy is kinetic: $\tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$, giving $v_{\max} = A\sqrt{k/m} = A\omega_0$.

Check

Matches the SHM solution $x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t)$, with $v(t) = -A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0 t)$, maximum $A\omega_0$ ✓.

Example

A pendulum of length $L$ is released from angle $\theta_0$ (small or not). Find its speed at the bottom. (Energy conservation applied to the pendulum setup from 2, §14.5.1.)

  1. Height of bob above the bottom: $h(\theta) = L(1 - \cos\theta)$.

  2. At release: $v_0 = 0$, $U_0 = mgL(1 - \cos\theta_0)$. At the bottom: $U = 0$.

  3. $\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgL(1 - \cos\theta_0)$, so $v = \sqrt{2gL(1 - \cos\theta_0)}$.

Check

For small $\theta_0$: $1 - \cos\theta_0 \approx \theta_0^2/2$, so $v \approx \theta_0\sqrt{gL}$. Compare with the SHM result for a simple pendulum: amplitude $\theta_0$, angular frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$, max tangential speed $L\theta_0\omega_0 = \theta_0\sqrt{gL}$ ✓.
