Two-Body Problem and Reduced Mass

A system of two bodies interacting only with each other reduces to the motion of a single fictitious particle of reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) in the given potential.

In short

Two things pulling on each other — like Earth and the Moon, or two electrons — is a hard problem: six coordinates, two moving bodies.

The trick: change variables 2.

  • The centre of mass $\mathbf R$ moves at constant velocity (no external force). So go to its frame; now it sits still.
  • The relative position $\mathbf r = \mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1$ carries all the interesting motion.

In the CM frame, $\mathbf r$ moves as if it were a single fictitious particle of the reduced mass 2:

$$\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

$\mu$ is always smaller than either $m_1$ or $m_2$. For equal masses, $\mu = m/2$. For a very light particle orbiting a heavy one (electron around proton, planet around Sun), $\mu$ is nearly the light mass.

What does this buy you? You can now use all the one-body tools of Chapter 3 (energy, angular momentum, central-force motion). Everything that followed about the gravitational potential energy, Kepler's laws, circular orbits — it was really all done with $\mu$ in place of $m$ 2.

For the Sun-planet system, the Sun is a million times heavier than the Earth, so $\mu \approx m_{\text{Earth}}$ — the Sun stays still to very high accuracy. For a binary star with equal masses, both stars orbit the CM at the same distance.

Theory

The two-body problem

Two particles of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ interact only with each other through a potential $U(|\mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1|)$ depending only on their separation. Newton's laws for each 1 §Definition of Central Force; 2:

$$m_1\ddot{\mathbf r}_1 = -\nabla_1 U, \qquad m_2\ddot{\mathbf r}_2 = -\nabla_2 U.$$

Six coordinates — a mess until you change variables.

Centre of mass and relative coordinates

Change to 2:

$$\mathbf R = \frac{m_1\mathbf r_1 + m_2\mathbf r_2}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad \mathbf r = \mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1.$$

$\mathbf R$ is the centre of mass; $\mathbf r$ is the relative coordinate. Adding the two Newton equations: $M\ddot{\mathbf R} = 0$, where $M = m_1 + m_2$. So the centre of mass moves at constant velocity (no external force). You can go to the CM frame where $\mathbf R = \mathbf 0$ 2.

The reduced mass

Subtracting ($\ddot{\mathbf r}_2 - \ddot{\mathbf r}_1$) and simplifying 2:

$$\mu\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F(\mathbf r), \qquad \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

The reduced mass $\mu$ turns the two-body problem into a one-body problem: a fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ at position $\mathbf r$ in the force field $\mathbf F$. All the original dynamics are encoded in $\mathbf r(t)$ and the CM drift 2.

Kinetic energy and angular momentum

The kinetic energy splits cleanly 2:

$$K = \tfrac12 M\dot{\mathbf R}^2 + \tfrac12 \mu\dot{\mathbf r}^2.$$

The first term is the CM's bulk motion; the second is the "internal" motion of the reduced particle.

The total angular momentum about the CM is $\mathbf L = \mu\,\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}$. For a central force (always along $\mathbf r$), no torque acts on the reduced particle: $\mathbf L$ is conserved. This makes the reduced motion planar (in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf L$).

Limiting cases

Two useful limits:

  • $m_1 = m_2 = m$: $\mu = m/2$. Each body orbits the CM at the same distance.
  • $m_2 \gg m_1$: $\mu \approx m_1$. The reduced motion is essentially that of the lighter body around the heavier one — this is the Sun-planet case.

Note

The reduced mass is always less than either individual mass: $\mu < m_1$ and $\mu < m_2$. For equal masses it is half; for a very unequal pair it is close to the smaller mass 2.

Warning

$\mu\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F(\mathbf r)$ looks like Newton's second law for a single particle, but $\mathbf r$ is the relative position vector — not the position of either real body. The real bodies both move, in opposite senses, in the CM frame 2.

Visualization

Two bodies of mass $m_1$ (dark, bigger) and $m_2$ (grey, smaller) orbit their common centre of mass. In the CM frame, both go around at the same angular velocity, each at a distance from the CM proportional to the other body's mass: $d_1 = (m_2/M)r$, $d_2 = (m_1/M)r$. Equal masses: symmetric circles. Very unequal masses (e.g. Sun + Earth): the heavy body barely moves while the light one does almost all the orbiting.

Worked examples

Example

Two equal stars in a binary each of mass $m$ orbit the CM at radius $R$ with angular velocity $\omega$. Find the gravitational force law and the Kepler-type relation. (Based on the reduced-mass formalism of 2.)

  1. Separation: $r = 2R$. Reduced mass: $\mu = m/2$.

  2. Reduced-particle circular motion: $\mu\omega^2 r = Gm^2/r^2$, i.e. $(m/2)\omega^2(2R) = Gm^2/(2R)^2$.

  3. Solve: $\omega^2 = Gm/(4R^3)$, so $T^2 = 16\pi^2 R^3/(Gm)$.

Check

Reality check: each star traces a circle of radius $R$ at angular velocity $\omega$, with the gravitational force $Gm^2/(2R)^2$ providing the centripetal force $m\omega^2 R$ ✓.

Example

Earth-Sun: $m_1 = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$, $m_2 = 1.99\times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$. Find the reduced mass and show it's essentially $m_1$. (Direct from 2.)

  1. $\mu = m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2) \approx m_1(1 - m_1/m_2)$.

  2. $m_1/m_2 \approx 3\times 10^{-6}$, so $\mu \approx m_1$ to 6 significant figures.

Check

That's why pretending the Sun is fixed and Earth orbits around it gives a very accurate answer ✓.

Example

A Hydrogen atom: $m_p = 1836\,m_e$. Find the reduced mass of the electron-proton system in units of $m_e$. (Direct from 2; this correction appears in the Rydberg constant for hydrogen.)

  1. $\mu = m_e m_p/(m_e + m_p) = m_e \cdot 1836/1837$.

  2. $\mu \approx 0.99946\,m_e$.

Check

0.054% correction. Small, but measurable in atomic spectra — it shifts hydrogen lines relative to what you'd compute with an infinitely heavy proton ✓.
