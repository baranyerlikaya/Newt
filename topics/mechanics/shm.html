Simple Harmonic Motion

A mass on a spring released from rest oscillates forever: x(t) = R cos(ω₀t + φ). The equation ẍ + ω₀²x = 0 and its solution, the amplitude-phase form, period and frequency.

In short

Mass on a spring, released and left alone. It bounces back and forth forever (no friction yet). That's simple harmonic motion (SHM).

The equation of motion is 1:

$$\ddot x = -\omega_0^2 x, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}.$$

The acceleration is always pointing back toward the centre, and it's biggest when you're farthest away.

The answer is a cosine 1:

$$x(t) = R\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi).$$
  • Amplitude $R$: how far it swings. Set by how you let go.
  • Phase $\phi$: where in the cycle you start the clock.
  • Angular frequency $\omega_0$: how fast it swings. Set by the spring and mass only — stiff spring, light mass → faster 1.

Period (time for one full bounce): $T = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ 1.

The big surprise: the period doesn't depend on the amplitude. Push the spring a lot or a little — same beat.

Pendulum: same equation, $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$. A 1 m pendulum ticks about every 2 s 1.

Everything oscillates like this near equilibrium. Any dip in a potential energy curve looks like a tiny parabola from close up — and parabolas give springs 1; 2.

Theory

The equation of motion

A mass $m$ on a spring of force constant $k$ (with equilibrium at $x = 0$) feels a restoring force $F = -kx$. Newton's second law gives the simple harmonic oscillator equation 1 §Equation of Motion for Simple Oscillations:

$$\ddot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}.$$

The quantity $\omega_0$, the natural angular frequency, is determined by the system alone (not by how you start it) 1.

Solution via e^{λt}

Try $x = e^{\lambda t}$. Plugging in gives the characteristic equation $\lambda^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0$, with roots $\lambda = \pm i\omega_0$ 1 §Solution to the Equation of Motion. The two complex solutions $e^{\pm i\omega_0 t}$ combine into a real two-parameter family:

$$x(t) = C\cos\omega_0 t + D\sin\omega_0 t.$$

Initial conditions $x(0) = x_0$, $\dot x(0) = v_0$ fix $C = x_0$ and $D = v_0/\omega_0$ 1:

$$x(t) = x_0\cos\omega_0 t + \frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\sin\omega_0 t.$$

Amplitude-phase form

The general solution can also be written as 1 §Amplitude-Phase Form:

$$x(t) = R\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi),$$

where the amplitude $R$ and initial phase $\phi$ are set by the initial conditions 1:

$$R = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \frac{v_0^2}{\omega_0^2}}, \qquad \tan\phi = -\frac{v_0}{\omega_0 x_0}.$$

$R$ is the maximum displacement from equilibrium; $\phi$ sets "where in the cycle" you start the clock 1.

Period and frequency

The motion repeats after the period $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$. The ordinary frequency (hertz) is $f = 1/T = \omega_0/(2\pi)$ 1 §Physical Interpretation of the General Solution. Neither depends on the amplitude — stiffer spring or lighter mass oscillates faster, but a bigger swing doesn't change the beat.

Where SHM shows up

Any system sitting at a stable equilibrium, when disturbed a little, oscillates approximately with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m}$ 1 §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium — a point we developed in Topic 5.5. Hence pendulums, molecules in a bond, and bridges in the wind all share this same equation 2 §14.6.

For the simple pendulum of length $L$: expanding gravity to first order in the angle, $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\theta = 0$, so $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ and the period is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ 1 Worked Example "Simple pendulum".

Note

The initial conditions set $R$ and $\phi$; the physics sets $\omega_0$ 1. Two different initial conditions give two different oscillations with exactly the same beat.

Warning

$\omega_0$ is called angular frequency and lives in rad/s. The ordinary frequency $f$ lives in Hz. They differ by a factor of $2\pi$ 1.

Visualization

A mass on a spring released from $x = 1$. With friction $b = 0$ it is a simple harmonic oscillator: $x(t) = \cos(\omega_0 t)$, a perfect cosine whose period is $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$. $\omega_0$ shown in the top-right corner changes when you change $k$ or $m$, but nothing changes the amplitude or beat of a given run. Try $k = 12$, $m = 1$: $\omega_0 \approx 3.46\ \text{rad/s}$, so $T \approx 1.8\ \text{s}$.

Worked examples

Example

A mass on a spring is released from rest at $x = x_0$. Find $x(t)$, the maximum speed and the period. (From 1, §Solution to the Equation of Motion and §Amplitude-Phase Form.)

  1. Initial conditions: $x(0) = x_0$, $\dot x(0) = 0$. Then $C = x_0$, $D = 0$, so $x(t) = x_0\cos\omega_0 t$.

  2. Maximum speed at $x = 0$: $|v|_{\max} = R\omega_0 = x_0\omega_0$.

  3. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, independent of $x_0$.

Check

Larger mass → slower oscillation (bigger $T$), stiffer spring → faster, but you can double the amplitude without changing the period at all ✓.

Example

A simple pendulum of length $L = 1\ \text{m}$ is released from $\theta_0 = 0.1\ \text{rad}$ at rest. Find its period and the amplitude of $\theta(t)$. (From 1, Worked Example "Simple pendulum".)

  1. Small-angle equation: $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\theta = 0$, so $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L} \approx \sqrt{9.81} \approx 3.13\ \text{rad/s}$.

  2. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega_0 \approx 2.01\ \text{s}$.

  3. $\theta(0) = 0.1$, $\dot\theta(0) = 0$, so $\theta(t) = 0.1\cos(3.13\,t)$: amplitude $0.1\ \text{rad}$.

Check

For small amplitudes the period depends only on $L$ and $g$. A pendulum clock with a $1\ \text{m}$ bob ticks about every second, matching the standard-pendulum design ✓.

Example

For $U(x) = k(x^3 - 3x)$, find the small-oscillation frequency about the stable equilibrium. (From Topic 5.5 Example 1 and 1, §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium.)

  1. Stable equilibrium at $x_0 = 1$, with $U''(1) = 6k$.

  2. Small oscillations: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m} = \sqrt{6k/m}$.

Check

Same shape as the spring result with the local "effective stiffness" $6k$. ✓
