Kepler's Laws

The three empirical laws of planetary motion and how they follow from the inverse-square law of gravity and conservation of angular momentum.

In short

Kepler's three laws describe how planets orbit the Sun. Johannes Kepler figured them out from data about 50 years before Newton proved why.

Law 1: Orbits are ellipses, with the Sun at one focus 1. Not perfect circles. Earth's is very close to circular; Pluto's is noticeably elongated; comets' can be extremely stretched.

Law 2: Equal areas in equal times 1. Draw a line from the Sun to a planet. In any fixed amount of time, that line sweeps out the same area, no matter where the planet is in its orbit. Consequence: planets move fast when they're close to the Sun, slow when they're far. This is really the conservation of angular momentum in disguise.

Law 3: $T^2 \propto a^3$ 1. The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the orbit size. In AU and years with the Sun, it is simply $T^2 = a^3$:

  • Earth: $a = 1$ AU, $T = 1$ year ✓.
  • Mars: $a \approx 1.52$, so $T^2 = 3.5$, $T \approx 1.88$ years ✓.
  • Neptune: $a = 30$, $T^2 = 27000$, $T \approx 164$ years ✓.

All three laws come out of two things we already have: the inverse-square force and conservation of angular momentum 1.

Theory

Law 1: Elliptical orbits

Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.

The orbit equation, derived from the inverse-square force, is

$$r(\theta) = \frac{a(1-e^2)}{1 + e\cos\theta},$$

where $a$ is the semi-major axis (average of perihelion $r_{\min} = a(1-e)$ and aphelion $r_{\max} = a(1+e)$), and $e$ is the eccentricity: $0 \le e < 1$ for an ellipse (circle at $e = 0$), $e = 1$ for a parabola, $e > 1$ for a hyperbola 1 §9.2.2.

Law 2: Equal areas in equal times

The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

In polar coordinates, the area swept in time $dt$ is $dA = \tfrac12 r^2 d\theta$. So

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \tfrac12 r^2\dot\theta = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{const}.$$

This is simply conservation of angular momentum ($L = mr^2\dot\theta$) — exactly what you'd expect for any central force. Kepler's second law is not special to inverse-square, it holds for any central force 1 §9.1.

Consequence: planets move fast near perihelion (close to the Sun) and slow near aphelion.

Law 3: T² ∝ a³

The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.

For a circular orbit of radius $r$ around a mass $M$, $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$ (Topic 7.2), so $T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$. Squaring 1 §9.2.2:

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3.$$

For ellipses the same result holds with $r$ replaced by the semi-major axis $a$. The proportionality constant $4\pi^2/(GM)$ is the same for all bodies orbiting the same $M$: Mercury and Neptune share this law 1.

In practical astronomical units (AU, years, solar mass), $T^2 = a^3$ exactly.

Energy and eccentricity

For a Kepler orbit, the total energy is

$$E = -\frac{GMm}{2a}.$$

Negative for bound orbits ($a > 0$, ellipse). The eccentricity is related to the angular momentum by $e = \sqrt{1 + 2EL^2/(m(GMm)^2)}$. Circular orbit ($e = 0$) corresponds to the minimum-energy orbit for a given $L$.

Note

Kepler's laws were derived from Tycho Brahe's data decades before Newton. Newton then showed that laws 1 and 3 require inverse-square gravity. Any other power law would give orbits that don't close after one turn 1.

Warning

The Sun is at one focus of each ellipse, not the centre. For a nearly circular orbit the difference is tiny; for a very elongated comet orbit, it is huge 1.

Visualization

A planet on an elliptical orbit with the "Sun" at one focus. The shaded wedge is the area swept by the Sun-planet line over a short time interval. Even as you change the eccentricity $e$, the rate at which area is swept stays constant (Kepler's second law): the planet races near the focus and dawdles far away. $e = 0$ is a circle (constant speed); larger $e$ stretches the orbit and increases the speed contrast.

Worked examples

Example

Verify Kepler's third law for Mercury ($a = 5.79\times 10^{10}\ \text{m}$) and Neptune ($a = 4.50\times 10^{12}\ \text{m}$) using $M_{\odot} = 1.99\times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$. (Standard application of 1.)

  1. $T^2 = (4\pi^2/GM_{\odot})\,a^3$. Let $C = 4\pi^2/(GM_{\odot}) \approx 2.97\times 10^{-19}\ \text{s}^2/\text{m}^3$.

  2. Mercury: $T = \sqrt{C\cdot(5.79\times 10^{10})^3} \approx 7.60\times 10^6\ \text{s} \approx 88.0\ \text{days}$.

  3. Neptune: $T = \sqrt{C\cdot(4.50\times 10^{12})^3} \approx 5.20\times 10^9\ \text{s} \approx 165\ \text{years}$.

Check

Mercury's orbital period is about 88 days ✓. Neptune's is about 165 years ✓.

Example

A comet is in an elliptical orbit around the Sun with perihelion $r_{\min} = 0.5\ \text{AU}$ and aphelion $r_{\max} = 20\ \text{AU}$. If its perihelion speed is $v_{\min}$-side 60 km/s, find the aphelion speed using Kepler's 2nd law. (Application of angular momentum conservation.)

  1. At perihelion and aphelion, velocity is perpendicular to $\mathbf r$. So $L = mvr$ gives $v_{\text{per}}r_{\text{per}} = v_{\text{aph}}r_{\text{aph}}$.

  2. $v_{\text{aph}} = v_{\text{per}}\cdot r_{\text{per}}/r_{\text{aph}} = 60 \times 0.5/20 = 1.5\ \text{km/s}$.

Check

Moves 40× faster near the Sun. Area per time: $\tfrac12(0.5)(60) = \tfrac12(20)(1.5) = 15$ (AU·km/s). Equal areas ✓.

Example

What is the orbital period of a geostationary satellite, and what radius does it orbit at? ($T = 1\ \text{sidereal day} = 86164\ \text{s}$; use $GM_E = 3.98\times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$.)

  1. From $T^2 = (4\pi^2/GM_E)\,a^3$, solve for $a$: $a = (GM_E T^2/(4\pi^2))^{1/3}$.

  2. $GM_E T^2 = 3.98\times 10^{14} \times (86164)^2 \approx 2.95\times 10^{24}$.

  3. $a = (2.95\times 10^{24}/39.48)^{1/3} \approx (7.48\times 10^{22})^{1/3} \approx 4.22\times 10^7\ \text{m}$.

Check

Height above Earth: $a - R_E \approx 35,800\ \text{km}$, which matches geostationary (GEO) altitude ✓.
