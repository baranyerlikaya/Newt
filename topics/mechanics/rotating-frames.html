Rotating Frames and Centrifugal Force

In a frame rotating with angular velocity Ω, Newton's second law gains two fictitious forces: centrifugal F_cf = +mΩ²r outward, and Coriolis F_Cor = −2mΩ×v'.

In short

In a rotating frame (a merry-go-round, Earth's surface), Newton's laws don't just need the one fictitious force from linear acceleration — they need two 1:

  • Centrifugal: $\mathbf F_{\text{cf}} = m\Omega^2\mathbf r_\perp$ pointing outward from the axis. Felt whether you're moving or not.
  • Coriolis: $\mathbf F_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'$. Only felt when you move in the rotating frame. Perpendicular to your motion.

Centrifugal is the "thrown outward" feeling on a merry-go-round. It's really just your inertia — you want to go straight, the floor curves under you.

Coriolis bends moving things to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern. It doesn't speed things up or slow them down; it only turns them.

Earth's rotation makes you about $0.3\%$ lighter at the equator than at the poles, because centrifugal pulls you outward (opposing gravity) 1.

Next page (Coriolis & Earth): hurricanes, trade winds, long-range artillery, Foucault's pendulum — all consequences of Coriolis.

Theory

Rotating frame kinematics

For a frame rotating about a fixed axis with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega$, the derivative of any vector $\mathbf Q$ in the rotating frame differs from its derivative in the inertial frame by 1 §31.4, Appendix 31.A:

$$\left(\frac{d\mathbf Q}{dt}\right)_{\text{inertial}} = \left(\frac{d\mathbf Q}{dt}\right)_{\text{rot}} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf Q.$$

Applying this twice to the position vector gives the acceleration relation 1 §31.4.2:

$$\mathbf a_{\text{inertial}} = \mathbf a' + 2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r),$$

where $\mathbf v'$ and $\mathbf a'$ are velocity and acceleration measured in the rotating frame.

Fictitious forces

Rewrite Newton's second law in the rotating frame: $m\mathbf a' = \mathbf F_{\text{physical}} - m\,2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v' - m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$, so 1 §31.4.2:

$$\mathbf F_{\text{physical}} + \mathbf F_{\text{Coriolis}} + \mathbf F_{\text{centrifugal}} = m\mathbf a',$$

with

$$\mathbf F_{\text{Coriolis}} = -2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v',$$ $$\mathbf F_{\text{centrifugal}} = -m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).$$

Centrifugal force

For $\boldsymbol\Omega$ along $\hat z$ and $\mathbf r_\perp$ the perpendicular distance from the axis 1 §31.5.2:

$$\mathbf F_{\text{cf}} = +m\Omega^2\,\mathbf r_\perp.$$

Points outward, perpendicular to the rotation axis, and grows linearly with distance. It doesn't depend on the body's motion in the rotating frame.

In a merry-go-round, this is the "throwing outward" you feel — really just your inertia wanting to go straight while the floor turns under you 1.

Coriolis force

$\mathbf F_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'$ acts only on bodies that are moving in the rotating frame. It is perpendicular to $\mathbf v'$ (like a magnetic force), so it doesn't change the speed, only the direction 1 §31.5.3.

In the Northern Hemisphere ($\boldsymbol\Omega$ pointing out of the ground), it deflects a moving body to the right. In the Southern Hemisphere, to the left. We will see consequences in Topic 10.3 (Motion on Earth).

A useful rewrite for astronomy

For a body at rest in the rotating frame ($\mathbf v' = 0$), only centrifugal acts. For a body moving through the rotating frame, both do. This is why rotating-frame physics is more tangled than linearly-accelerating-frame physics.

If the rotating frame's axis also moves (orbits), you get more terms; the full treatment is in 1.

Note

In the rotating frame of the Earth, the centrifugal force gives an "effective" gravity slightly smaller than $GM_E/R_E^2$, and tilted away from the rotation axis. Measurements of $g$ at the equator vs the pole reveal this correction (plus Earth's oblateness) 1 §31.5.2, Example 6.

Warning

"Centrifugal" and "centripetal" are opposite in direction. Centripetal is the real inward force (e.g., tension) in the inertial frame; centrifugal is the fictitious outward force in the rotating frame. Don't use both at once 1.

Visualization

A puck released on a rotating turntable. In the turntable's frame, the Coriolis force bends its path. Increase $\Omega$ (faster rotation): stronger deflection. Set $\Omega = 0$: the puck moves in a straight line, no fictitious force, the frame is inertial.

Worked examples

Example

A mass sits at rest on a rotating platform at radius $r$. In the rotating frame, what force keeps it at rest? (From 1, Example 4.)

  1. In the rotating frame $\mathbf v' = 0$, so only centrifugal acts: $\mathbf F_{\text{cf}} = +m\Omega^2 r$ outward.

  2. Physical force: static friction from the platform, inward, with magnitude $m\Omega^2 r$.

  3. In the inertial frame: friction supplies the centripetal force needed to keep the mass going in a circle. Same physics, different accounting.

Check

Maximum $r$ before sliding: $\mu_s mg \ge m\Omega^2 r$, so $r_{\max} = \mu_s g/\Omega^2$ ✓.

Example

A puck slides outward on a frictionless rotating turntable with speed $v'$ (in the turntable frame). What's the Coriolis force on it? (From 1, Example 2.)

  1. $\boldsymbol\Omega = \Omega\hat z$ (axis of turntable). $\mathbf v' = v'\hat r$ (outward in the rotating frame).

  2. $\mathbf F_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v' = -2m\Omega v'(\hat z\times\hat r) = -2m\Omega v'\hat\theta$.

  3. Points opposite to the direction of rotation (clockwise if the turntable rotates CCW). The puck appears to curve backward as it slides outward — in the inertial frame, it goes in a straight line and the turntable rotates under it.

Check

Coriolis is perpendicular to $\mathbf v'$: it doesn't change the puck's speed in the turntable frame, just bends its path ✓.

Example

Correction to $g$ at the equator due to Earth's rotation. $\Omega = 2\pi/86400 \approx 7.27\times 10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s}$, $R_E = 6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$. (From 1, Example 6.)

  1. At the equator, centrifugal is radially outward with magnitude $\Omega^2 R_E$ per unit mass.

  2. $\Omega^2 R_E = (7.27\times 10^{-5})^2 \times 6.37\times 10^6 \approx 0.0337\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  3. So $g_{\text{eff}} \approx 9.81 - 0.034 \approx 9.78\ \text{m/s}^2$ at the equator — about $0.3\%$ smaller than at the pole.

Check

Measured: $g_{\text{eq}} \approx 9.78$, $g_{\text{pole}} \approx 9.83\ \text{m/s}^2$ — the extra 0.02 comes from Earth's oblate shape ✓.
