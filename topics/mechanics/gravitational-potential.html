Gravitational Potential Energy and Escape Velocity

For the inverse-square gravity, U(r) = -GMm/r. Energy conservation then gives the escape velocity from a planet and the energies of circular and elliptical orbits.

In short

Gravity has a potential energy — the "stored energy" of being far from a planet 1:

$$U(r) = -\frac{GMm}{r}.$$

Why negative? We pick the convention $U = 0$ at infinity (very far away). Gravity is attractive, so bringing the body in from infinity releases energy — which means $U$ gets smaller (more negative) as $r$ gets smaller. The negative sign is a consequence of this reference choice, not of gravity itself.

Near Earth's surface, this reduces to the familiar $U = mgh$ — first-order Taylor expansion of the full formula.

Escape velocity. To leave a planet and not come back, you need enough speed that $\tfrac12 mv^2 \ge GMm/R$. Set equal:

$$v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.$$

For Earth: about $11.2\ \text{km/s}$. For the Moon: $2.4\ \text{km/s}$. In this idealized budget (no air drag, no Earth's rotation, only gravity) the direction doesn't matter — only the speed does, because energy is a scalar 1. Real rockets exploit Earth's rotation by launching eastward (saving up to about $0.46\ \text{km/s}$ at the equator) and fight air drag below the atmosphere.

To orbit at radius $r$, you need less — just enough centripetal force: $v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{GM/r} = v_e/\sqrt 2$ at the surface. ISS orbits at about $7.7\ \text{km/s}$.

Total orbit energy: for a circular orbit, $E = -GMm/(2r)$. Negative = bound. To raise a satellite to a higher orbit, you must add energy, even though it moves slower up there (less kinetic energy, but even less potential).

Theory

Gravitational potential energy

The force $F_r = -GMm/r^2$ is conservative (it is radial and depends only on $r$). Integrate $F_r = -dU/dr$ with the reference $U(\infty) = 0$ 1 §14.4.3:

$$U(r) = -\frac{GMm}{r}.$$

$U < 0$ everywhere, going to $0$ at infinity and $-\infty$ at $r = 0$. The bigger the separation, the less negative (so higher) the energy.

Near Earth's surface: U ≈ mgh

Expand $U$ around $r = R_E$: for a particle at height $h$ with $h \ll R_E$ 1 §14.4.1:

$$\begin{aligned}U(R_E + h) &= -\frac{GMm}{R_E}\left(1 - \frac{h}{R_E} + \cdots\right) \\ &\approx \text{const} + mgh,\end{aligned}$$

with $g = GM/R_E^2$ as before. So "$U = mgh$" is the first-order expansion of the inverse-square potential.

Total energy of a bound orbit

For a particle of mass $m$ at distance $r$ from $M$ moving with speed $v$ 1 §14.5:

$$E = \tfrac12 mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r}.$$

Energy conservation along the orbit links speed to distance:

$$\tfrac12 v_1^2 - \frac{GM}{r_1} = \tfrac12 v_2^2 - \frac{GM}{r_2}.$$

Escape velocity

To escape to infinity (where $U = 0$), you need $E \ge 0$. The minimum is $E = 0$ at launch:

$$\tfrac12 v_e^2 - \frac{GM}{R} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.$$

From Earth's surface, $v_e \approx 11.2\ \text{km/s}$. From the Moon's surface, $\approx 2.4\ \text{km/s}$. Independent of the launch direction (since only speed matters in the energy budget) 1.

Energy of a circular orbit

For a circular orbit of radius $r$, gravity supplies the centripetal force: $GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r$, so $v^2 = GM/r$ and $K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/(2r)$. Then

$$E = K + U = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}.$$

Negative — bound. Note that $|U| = 2K$ for a circular orbit (a special case of the virial theorem).

Note

$|E|$ decreases as $r$ grows: higher orbits have more total energy (less negative). To move a satellite up, you must add energy, even though it ends up moving slower 1.

Warning

Escape velocity gives you enough energy to just barely reach infinity with zero speed. Real rockets launch with a slight excess. And escape velocity is not the velocity needed to orbit — that's smaller, $v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{GM/R} = v_e/\sqrt2$ 1.

Worked examples

Example

Find Earth's escape velocity. (Standard application of 1.)

  1. $v_e = \sqrt{2GM_E/R_E} = \sqrt{2(6.674\times 10^{-11})(5.97\times 10^{24})/(6.37\times 10^6)}$.

  2. Inside root: $\approx 1.25\times 10^8$, so $v_e \approx 1.12\times 10^4\ \text{m/s} \approx 11.2\ \text{km/s}$.

Check

Equivalently $v_e = \sqrt{2gR_E} = \sqrt{2\times 9.81\times 6.37\times 10^6} \approx 11.2\ \text{km/s}$ ✓.

Example

What speed does a satellite need to circle at the ISS altitude ($h = 400\ \text{km}$)? (Application of $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$.)

  1. $r = R_E + h = 6.77\times 10^6\ \text{m}$.

  2. $v = \sqrt{GM/r} \approx \sqrt{3.98\times 10^{14}/6.77\times 10^6} \approx 7.67\ \text{km/s}$.

  3. Orbital period: $T = 2\pi r/v \approx 5.55\times 10^3\ \text{s} \approx 92\ \text{minutes}$.

Check

Matches the ISS's real 90-minute orbit ✓. Much smaller than the $11.2\ \text{km/s}$ escape speed: $v = v_e/\sqrt2$ exactly for circular orbit near the surface.

Example

Show that the energy of a circular orbit of radius $r$ is $-GMm/(2r)$. (Direct derivation from 1.)

  1. Circular condition: $v^2 = GM/r$, so $K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/(2r)$.

  2. Add $U$: $E = GMm/(2r) - GMm/r = -GMm/(2r)$.

Check

$E$ is negative (bound), and $|U|/K = 2$ — the virial relation for inverse-square forces. ✓
