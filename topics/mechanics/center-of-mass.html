Centre of Mass

For a system of N particles, the centre of mass R = Σmᵢrᵢ/M is the mass-weighted average position. Its motion captures the system's bulk translation.

In short

The centre of mass is the "average location" of a bunch of mass, weighted by how much mass is where 1:

$$\mathbf R = \frac{\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i}{M}.$$

For a heavy object and a light object, the CM is close to the heavy one.

For uniform symmetric shapes (sphere, cube, rod, disc), the CM is at the geometric centre. A hammer's CM is up near the head because most of its mass is there.

Why we care: if you throw a complicated spinning object (like a wrench) in the air, its CM traces a simple parabola — just as if the whole thing were a point mass. All the complicated spinning happens around the CM 1.

The system's momentum is just $\mathbf p = M\mathbf V_{\text{CM}}$. For external forces: $\mathbf F_{\text{ext}} = M\mathbf A_{\text{CM}}$. Newton's second law written for the CM looks exactly like it does for a single particle — regardless of how many pieces are inside 1.

Earth-Moon: their CM is about 1700 km below Earth's surface. So as the Moon goes around Earth, Earth really wobbles slightly around a point inside itself.

Theory

Definition

The centre of mass of $N$ particles is 1 §10.5:

$$\mathbf R = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\mathbf r_i, \qquad M = \sum_i m_i.$$

It is a mass-weighted average: particles of larger mass pull $\mathbf R$ toward them. For a continuous body 1 §10.5:

$$\mathbf R = \frac{1}{M}\int\mathbf r\,dm.$$

Where it is for common shapes

For uniform bodies, the CM is at the geometric centre of symmetry 1 §10.5, Example 10.3:

  • Uniform rod: midpoint.
  • Uniform disc, sphere: centre.
  • Uniform triangle: intersection of the medians (not centroid, but same place).

For two bodies, the CM lies on the line joining them at distances $d_1 = m_2 r/(m_1+m_2)$ from body 1 and $d_2 = m_1 r/(m_1+m_2)$ from body 2 (inverse mass ratio) 1.

Velocity and momentum

Differentiate: the CM velocity is 1 §10.5

$$\mathbf V_{\text{CM}} = \dot{\mathbf R} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i = \frac{\mathbf p_{\text{sys}}}{M}.$$

So the total momentum of the system equals its mass times the CM velocity 1. If you know only the CM motion, you know the total momentum — everything else is internal.

Kinetic energy split

Decompose each particle's velocity as $\mathbf v_i = \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} + \mathbf v_i'$, where $\mathbf v_i'$ is the velocity relative to the CM. The kinetic energy splits 1 §10.5:

$$K = \tfrac12 M V_{\text{CM}}^2 + K',$$

where $K' = \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i v_i'^2$ is the kinetic energy measured in the CM frame. Bulk motion plus internal motion — never mixed.

Reference bodies for continuous mass

For a body of continuous mass density $\rho(\mathbf r)$ 1 §10.5, Example 10.3:

$$\mathbf R = \frac{1}{M}\int\mathbf r\,\rho(\mathbf r)\,dV.$$

For a uniform rod of length $L$ along the $x$-axis: $R_x = \int_0^L x\,(\lambda\,dx)/M = L/2$.

Note

The centre of mass of two or more rigid bodies stuck together is found by putting each body's full mass at its own CM, then taking the weighted average — you don't have to re-integrate over the whole shape 1.

Warning

CM is not the same as the "geometric centre" for non-uniform bodies. A hammer's CM is near the head, not in the middle of the handle, because most of the mass is in the head 1.

Visualization

Two bodies orbit their common centre of mass. The CM is the dot in the middle; neither body is at it (unless they have equal mass). Lighter body → bigger orbit; heavier body → smaller orbit around the CM. The product $m\times d$ is the same for both.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Earth-Moon system: $m_E = 5.97\times 10^{24}$, $m_M = 7.35\times 10^{22}$, separation $3.84\times 10^8\ \text{m}$. Where is the CM? (From 1, Example 10.2.)

  1. Mass ratio: $m_M/(m_E+m_M) = 7.35/604 \approx 0.0122$.

  2. Distance from Earth's centre to CM: $0.0122\times 3.84\times 10^8 \approx 4.67\times 10^6\ \text{m}$.

  3. Earth's radius is $6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$, so the CM is inside the Earth, about 1700 km below its surface.

Check

Earth-Moon rotation is about that point, not about Earth's centre. Earth "wobbles" slightly as the Moon orbits ✓.

Example

Uniform rod of mass $M$ and length $L$: find the position of its CM. (From 1, Example 10.3.)

  1. Linear density $\lambda = M/L$.

  2. $R = \int_0^L x\,\lambda\,dx/M = (\lambda L^2/2)/M = L/2$.

  3. The midpoint, as expected by symmetry.

Check

The symmetry of a uniform rod means the CM must lie on the axis of symmetry — the geometric centre ✓.

Example

Two bodies, $m_1 = 1\ \text{kg}$ at $\mathbf r_1 = \hat\imath$ and $m_2 = 3\ \text{kg}$ at $\mathbf r_2 = 5\hat\imath$. Find the CM.

  1. $M = 4\ \text{kg}$. $\mathbf R = (1\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 5)\hat\imath/4 = 16\hat\imath/4 = 4\hat\imath$.

  2. CM is 1 unit from $m_2$ (the heavier body) and 3 units from $m_1$ — inverse mass ratio.

Check

$d_1 = m_2(r_2-r_1)/(m_1+m_2) = 3\cdot 4/4 = 3$ ✓, and $d_2 = m_1(r_2-r_1)/(m_1+m_2) = 1$ ✓.
