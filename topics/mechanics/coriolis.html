Coriolis Force and Motion on Earth

On Earth's rotating surface the Coriolis force bends moving things sideways. It shapes trade winds, hurricanes and ocean currents, and makes Foucault's pendulum slowly rotate its plane of swing.

In short

Earth rotates once a day. In the frame that rotates with it (the ground), things that move get pushed sideways by the Coriolis force 1:

  • Northern Hemisphere: anything moving gets nudged to its right.
  • Southern Hemisphere: nudged to its left.
  • Equator: no horizontal Coriolis.

The effect is tiny per second but builds up over long distances.

Weather is Coriolis-shaped. Trade winds blow westward because air flowing toward the equator gets deflected. Hurricanes rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere (clockwise in the Southern) because air rushing into a low-pressure centre gets curled by Coriolis 1.

Foucault's pendulum (1851) is the most beautiful demonstration. A long, heavy pendulum left to swing slowly rotates its plane of swing — once per day at the poles, once per $\sim 34$ hours at mid-latitudes, never at the equator. In the stars' frame the pendulum's plane doesn't move; it's Earth that turns beneath it 1.

Common myth: bathtub drains don't reliably spin one way per hemisphere. Household-scale Coriolis is overwhelmed by the tap, the tub's shape, and whatever stirring was there before 1.

Long-range artillery, missiles, and rockets all need Coriolis corrections.

Theory

Coriolis on Earth

Earth rotates with $\boldsymbol\Omega$ along its axis, $\Omega = 2\pi/(86164\ \text{s}) \approx 7.3\times 10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s}$. At latitude $\lambda$ (north of equator), the vertical component is $\Omega\sin\lambda$ and the horizontal is $\Omega\cos\lambda$ 1 §31.5.3.

For a body moving horizontally (parallel to the local ground), only the vertical part of $\boldsymbol\Omega$ contributes to a horizontal Coriolis deflection:

$$\mathbf F_{\text{Cor, horizontal}} = -2m\,\Omega\sin\lambda\,(\hat z\times\mathbf v'_{\text{horiz}}).$$

This deflects the velocity to the right in the Northern Hemisphere ($\lambda > 0$) and to the left in the Southern ($\lambda < 0$). At the equator ($\lambda = 0$), there is no horizontal Coriolis deflection 1.

Trade winds and hurricanes

Air flowing from the tropics toward the equator is deflected to the right (Northern) and left (Southern), creating the westward trade winds 1 §31.5.3.

In a hurricane, air rushes inward toward a low-pressure centre. Coriolis bends this inflow — in the Northern Hemisphere it swirls counterclockwise (looking down), in the Southern Hemisphere clockwise 1 Examples 7–8. The rotation direction is a direct Coriolis signature.

Deflection of falling bodies

A body dropped from height $h$ at the equator lands slightly to the east of the plumb line: in the (inertial) frame of the stars, the top of the tower moves east faster than the ground beneath it, so the released body carries that extra eastward speed.

Horizontally, for a long-range projectile in the Northern Hemisphere, Coriolis bends it to the right. Artillery tables for long-range guns include this correction 1 §31.5.3.

Foucault's pendulum

Hang a long, heavy pendulum with low friction, give it a gentle push. Its plane of swing rotates relative to the ground, completing a full turn in time

$$T_{\text{Foucault}} = \frac{T_{\text{day}}}{\sin\lambda}.$$

At the North Pole ($\lambda = 90°$): one turn per sidereal day. At $45°$ latitude: one turn per ~34 hours. At the equator: never — the horizontal Coriolis force vanishes 1 §31.5.3.

In the inertial frame the pendulum's plane doesn't rotate at all; it's Earth that rotates under it. Foucault's 1851 demonstration in the Pantheon was the first direct laboratory evidence of Earth's rotation 1.

Everyday scale

Coriolis is tiny at everyday scales. The acceleration is $\sim 2\Omega v \sim 10^{-4}\,v$: a bullet at $v = 500\ \text{m/s}$ feels only $\sim 0.07\ \text{m/s}^2$. Over a short distance this is irrelevant; over a kilometre or more it matters a lot.

The folklore about bathtub drains spinning differently in each hemisphere is wrong — household-scale Coriolis is much smaller than any wobble from the tap or tub shape 1.

Note

Coriolis is a result of the frame's rotation, not of any physical interaction with Earth. In an inertial (non-rotating) frame it doesn't appear — the "sideways deflection" is just the moving body going straight while Earth turns under it 1.

Warning

The Coriolis effect strength depends on $\sin\lambda$ (latitude), not on $\cos\lambda$. It is maximum at the poles and zero at the equator 1.

Visualization

Inspired by the Foucault pendulum / turntable analogy: a particle sliding on a rotating platform (viewed in the rotating frame). As the platform spins, the Coriolis force curves the particle's path. Set $\Omega = 0$: the particle moves in a straight line. Turn up $\Omega$: the path curls — this is the same qualitative effect that sets large-scale weather patterns on Earth.

Worked examples

Example

A wind blows due south at $20\ \text{m/s}$ in Boston ($\lambda = 42°\text{N}$). What is the Coriolis acceleration? In which direction? (Standard; from 1, §31.5.3.)

  1. $a_{\text{Cor}} = 2\Omega\sin\lambda\,v = 2\times 7.27\times 10^{-5}\times 0.669\times 20 \approx 1.9\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  2. Northern Hemisphere → deflection to the right. A south-blowing wind is deflected to the west.

Check

Small on a per-second basis, but over hours and thousands of kilometres this sets the overall direction of pressure-driven winds ✓.

Example

Foucault pendulum period at Paris ($\lambda = 48.86°$). (From 1.)

  1. $T_{\text{Foucault}} = T_{\text{day}}/\sin\lambda = 86164/\sin(48.86°) \approx 86164/0.753 \approx 114{,}400\ \text{s}$.

  2. $\approx 31.8\ \text{hours}$ for one full turn.

Check

Foucault's original 1851 demonstration showed a plane rotation of about 11° per hour (360°/32 h ≈ 11.25°/h) ✓.

Example

Long-range gun fires a shell $20\ \text{km}$ due north at $v_0 = 800\ \text{m/s}$ from latitude $45°$. Estimate the sideways drift at impact. (Rough application of $a \approx 2\Omega\sin\lambda\,v$.)

  1. Flight time: $t \approx L/v_0 = 20{,}000/800 = 25\ \text{s}$ (horizontal, straight-line estimate).

  2. Coriolis acceleration: $a \approx 2\times 7.27\times 10^{-5}\times 0.707\times 800 \approx 0.082\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  3. Drift: $\tfrac12 at^2 \approx \tfrac12\times 0.082\times 625 \approx 26\ \text{m}$ to the east (northern hemisphere, firing north).

Check

~25 m drift over 20 km. Significant enough that long-range artillery tables include it ✓.
