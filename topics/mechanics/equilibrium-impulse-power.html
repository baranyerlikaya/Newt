Equilibrium, Impulse, Power

Equilibrium points and their stability from the potential energy, impulse as the change in momentum, and power as the rate of work.

In short

Three loose ends from everything so far.

1. Equilibrium = "flat spot on the potential energy". Place a ball at rest on a flat part of the hill and it stays there. More formally [1]:

$$\frac{dU}{dx} = 0.$$

Stable equilibrium: the bottom of a bowl. Nudge it, it rolls back. $U$ has a local minimum.

Unstable equilibrium: the top of a hill. Nudge it, it rolls away. $U$ has a local maximum.

Near a stable point, small motions are (approximately) simple harmonic, with frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m}$ [1]; [2].

2. Impulse = how much "oomph" a push gives you [1]; [3]:

$$J = \int F\,dt = \Delta p.$$

Short, hard punch or long, soft shove, same impulse means same change in momentum. Airbags lengthen $\Delta t$ to lower $F$ for the same $\Delta p$.

3. Power = how fast you deliver energy [1]:

$$P = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.$$

Climbing stairs faster doesn't cost more energy, but it demands more power. 1 watt = 1 joule per second.

Theory

Equilibrium

A point $x_0$ is an equilibrium of a conservative 1D system if the force there vanishes [1, §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium]:

$$F(x_0) = -\frac{dU}{dx}\bigg|_{x_0} = 0.$$

So equilibria are where $U(x)$ is flat (critical points). A particle placed at rest there stays at rest.

Stable or unstable?

Look at the second derivative of $U$ [1, §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium]:

  • $U''(x_0) > 0$ (local minimum): displaced particle is pushed back — stable.
  • $U''(x_0) < 0$ (local maximum): displaced particle is pushed away — unstable.
  • $U''(x_0) = 0$: inconclusive from the second derivative; look at higher derivatives.

Near a stable equilibrium, Taylor-expand $U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \tfrac12 U''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2$: this is exactly a spring potential with $k = U''(x_0)$, so the small oscillations have angular frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m}$ [1]; [2, §14.6].

Impulse

The impulse of a force over a time interval is [1, §Impulse and Power]; [3, §10.2]

$$\mathbf J = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F(t)\,dt.$$

By Newton's second law, $\mathbf J = \Delta\mathbf p = \mathbf p_2 - \mathbf p_1$: impulse equals the change in momentum [1]. Geometrically, $|\mathbf J|$ is the area under the $|F|$-vs-$t$ graph [1]. The average force over the interval is

$$\mathbf F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\mathbf J}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf p}{\Delta t}.$$

Short, hard kicks (soccer, drum sticks) and long, soft pushes (rockets) can give the same impulse and the same change in momentum [3, §10.2].

Power

The power delivered by a force is the rate at which it does work [1, §Impulse and Power]:

$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.$$

Positive when force and velocity point the same way (energy flows in), negative when they oppose. Total work: $W = \int P\,dt$. Units: watt (W), with $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$. Legacy unit: $1\ \text{hp} \approx 735\ \text{W}$ [1, Worked Example “Horsepower”].

A single-particle energy diagram

Impulse, power, equilibrium and stability come together in the energy diagram: plot $U(x)$ and the horizontal line $E$. Equilibria are the critical points of $U$; stability is set by concavity; turning points are where $U = E$; the particle's speed at $x$ is $\sqrt{(2/m)(E - U(x))}$; and the time to go from $x_0$ to $x$ is the integral of $1/v$ [1, §More on the Potential Energy, §Mechanical Energy as a First Integral of Motion].

Note

Near any stable equilibrium (not just a spring), motion is approximately simple harmonic with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m}$. That is why so many small-oscillation problems in physics — pendulums, molecules, bridges, atoms — share the same mathematics [1, §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium]; [2, §14.6].

Warning

"Equilibrium" means no net force. It doesn't mean "at rest": a particle can pass through an equilibrium point with any speed. Only the special case $v = 0$ at equilibrium is a truly static situation [1, §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium].

Visualization

The potential $U(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$ shows a double well: two stable equilibria at $x = \pm 1$ (bottom of the wells) and one unstable equilibrium at $x = 0$ (top of the hump). The dashed line is the total energy $E$; the particle bounces between the turning points where $U = E$. Set $E < 0$: trapped in one well (small oscillation around $x = 1$, nearly SHM with $U'' = 12x^2 - 4$). Set $E > 0$: enough energy to cross the hump.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

$U(x) = k(x^3 - 3x)$ with $k > 0$. Find the equilibrium points, say which are stable, and the small-oscillation frequency about the stable one. (From [1], §More on the Potential Energy and §Stable and Unstable Equilibrium.)

  1. $U'(x) = k(3x^2 - 3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm1$.

  2. $U''(x) = 6kx$. At $x = 1$: $U''(1) = 6k > 0$ → stable. At $x = -1$: $U''(-1) = -6k < 0$ → unstable.

  3. Near $x = 1$: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{6k/m}$.

Check

$U(1) = -2k$ (bottom of the well), $U(-1) = 2k$ (top of the hump): the heights fit the picture of a well on the right and a bump on the left ✓.

Example

A soccer ball of mass $0.5\ \text{kg}$ is kicked with a force of $200\ \text{N}$ for $0.1\ \text{s}$. Find the impulse and the resulting speed (starting from rest). (From [1], Worked Example “Impulse — 1”.)

  1. $J = F\Delta t = 200 \times 0.1 = 20\ \text{N·s}$.

  2. $\Delta p = J = 20\ \text{kg·m/s}$.

  3. From rest: $v = \Delta p/m = 20/0.5 = 40\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

With $F = ma$: $a = 400\ \text{m/s}^2$, so after $0.1\ \text{s}$: $v = at = 40\ \text{m/s}$ ✓.

Example

A $1500\ \text{kg}$ car travelling at $10\ \text{m/s}$ hits a wall and stops in $0.2\ \text{s}$. Find the average force on the car. (From [1], Worked Example “Impulse — 2”.)

  1. $\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i) = 1500 \times (0 - 10) = -1.5\times10^4\ \text{kg·m/s}$.

  2. $F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p/\Delta t = -1.5\times10^4/0.2 = -7.5\times10^4\ \text{N} = -75\ \text{kN}$, opposite to the motion.

Check

Magnitude $75\ \text{kN}$ for 0.2 s delivers exactly $-15\ \text{kN·s}$ of impulse ✓.

Example

A $70\ \text{kg}$ person climbs $20\ \text{m}$ of stairs in $10\ \text{s}$. Find the average power delivered against gravity. (From [1], Worked Example “Power”.)

  1. Work against gravity: $W = mgh = 70 \times 9.8 \times 20 \approx 1.37\times10^4\ \text{J}$.

  2. Average power: $P = W/\Delta t = 1.37\times10^4/10 \approx 1.37\ \text{kW} \approx 1.9\ \text{hp}$.

Check

A 200 hp car engine delivers $200 \times 735 \approx 147\ \text{kW}$ — about 100 times more. ✓
