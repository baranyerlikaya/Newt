Rotational Inertia of a System of Particles

For a system of particles rotating together about an axis, the moment of inertia I = Σmᵢrᵢ² plays the role of mass, and the rotational kinetic energy is ½Iω².

In short

Mass for spinning. For straight-line motion, how hard something is to push depends on its mass $m$. For spinning, the same job is done by the moment of inertia $I$ — how hard it is to twist 2:

$$I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2.$$

Add up each piece of mass times the square of its distance from the axis. Far-out mass counts way more than close-in mass (it's squared).

Kinetic energy of spinning: swap the symbols in $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ for rotational ones 2:

$$K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2.$$

Angular momentum is also $I\omega$ for an object spinning about an axis 1; 3.

Figure-skater trick. Pull arms in → mass moves closer to the axis → $I$ drops → with no outside torque, $L = I\omega$ stays the same, so $\omega$ shoots up. That's why they spin faster 1; 2.

$I$ depends on which axis you spin about. A rod is much easier to spin around its length than around its middle.

Theory

A system of particles about a fixed axis

Consider $N$ particles rotating together about a fixed axis, each at perpendicular distance $r_i$ from the axis. Because the system is rigid, all share the same angular velocity $\omega$ 2 §16.3.1. Each particle moves on a circle at tangential speed $v_i = r_i\omega$.

Rotational kinetic energy

Add up each particle's kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 = \tfrac12 m_i r_i^2\omega^2$ 2 §16.3.1:

$$K_{\text{rot}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \tfrac12 m_i r_i^2 \omega^2 = \tfrac12\left(\sum_i m_i r_i^2\right)\omega^2.$$

The quantity in parentheses only depends on the geometry and masses, not on $\omega$:

The moment of inertia

The moment of inertia about the axis is 2 §16.3.1

$$\boxed{I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2.}$$

Units: kg·m². Then the rotational kinetic energy takes the simple form

$$K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2,$$

which is the rotational analogue of $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$. Here $I$ plays the role of mass and $\omega$ the role of velocity.

For a continuous body, the sum becomes an integral $I = \int r^2\,dm$ 2 §16.3.1. Simple results: a thin rod of mass $M$ and length $L$ about its centre, $I = ML^2/12$; a uniform disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about its symmetry axis, $I = \tfrac12MR^2$ 2 §16.3.2, Example 16.2.

Angular momentum of the system

For the same rigid rotation about a fixed axis, add up each particle's angular momentum component along the axis: $L_z = \sum_i m_i r_i v_i = \sum_i m_i r_i^2\omega = I\omega$. So 1 §Angular-Momentum Form of the 2nd Axiom; 3 §19.7

$$L_z = I\omega.$$

Rotational mass $I$ links angular velocity to angular momentum the way inertial mass links $\mathbf v$ to $\mathbf p$.

Depending on the axis

$I$ is not a property of the body alone; it depends on which axis you spin it about. Mass that is far from the axis counts quadratically (via $r^2$) and dominates. Pulling mass inward lowers $I$ without changing the masses themselves 1; 2 §16.3.1.

Note

$r_i$ in $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ is the perpendicular distance from the particle to the rotation axis, not the distance to a point. For a particle on the axis itself, its contribution is zero 2 §16.3.1.

Warning

$I$ is additive for a given axis: if two bodies rotate together about the same axis, $I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2$. It is not additive across different axes 2 §16.3.1.

Visualization

Four point masses (each 1 kg) on a cross, two at radius $r_1$ and two at radius $r_2$. The moment of inertia $I = 2m_1r_1^2 + 2m_2r_2^2$ and the rotational kinetic energy $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$ are shown live. Pull $r_1$ and $r_2$ inward and $I$ (and $K$) drop quickly — the $r^2$ dependence is dramatic. Change $\omega$ to see how $K$ scales as $\omega^2$.

Worked examples

Example

Two point masses $m$ each are fixed to the ends of a massless rod of length $2R$; the rod spins about its centre. Find $I$, $K_{\text{rot}}$ at angular velocity $\omega$, and $L$. (Direct application of 2, §16.3.1.)

  1. Each mass is at distance $R$ from the axis: $I = mR^2 + mR^2 = 2mR^2$.

  2. $K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = mR^2\omega^2$.

  3. $L = I\omega = 2mR^2\omega$ along the axis.

Check

Directly: each mass has $K_i = \tfrac12 m(R\omega)^2 = \tfrac12 mR^2\omega^2$; two of them give $mR^2\omega^2$ ✓.

Example

Four point masses of $1\ \text{kg}$ each sit at the corners of a $2\ \text{m} \times 2\ \text{m}$ square, connected by massless rods. Find the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre, perpendicular to the square. (Direct application of 2, §16.3.1.)

  1. Each mass is at distance $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt2\ \text{m}$ from the centre.

  2. $I = 4 \times 1 \times (\sqrt2)^2 = 8\ \text{kg·m}^2$.

Check

Half-size square (sides 1 m instead of 2): each $r = \sqrt{0.5}$, so $I = 4 \times 0.5 = 2\ \text{kg·m}^2$. Four times smaller area, four times smaller $I$ ✓ (since area scales as $r^2$).

Example

A skater has moment of inertia $I_1 = 5\ \text{kg·m}^2$ with arms outstretched and $I_2 = 2\ \text{kg·m}^2$ with arms pulled in. She starts spinning at $\omega_1 = 2\ \text{rev/s}$, then pulls her arms in. Find her final angular velocity and the change in rotational kinetic energy. (Based on 1, Worked Example “Conservation of angular momentum”; the example uses $I_f = I/2$.)

  1. No external torque: $L$ is conserved. $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$, so $\omega_2 = (I_1/I_2)\omega_1 = (5/2)\times 2 = 5\ \text{rev/s}$.

  2. $K_1 = \tfrac12 I_1\omega_1^2$, $K_2 = \tfrac12 I_2\omega_2^2 = \tfrac12 I_2(I_1/I_2)^2\omega_1^2 = (I_1/I_2)K_1 = 2.5 K_1$.

  3. Her kinetic energy grows by a factor $I_1/I_2 = 2.5$. The extra energy comes from the work her muscles do pulling the arms in 1 Worked Example “Conservation of angular momentum”.

Check

Angular momentum: $5 \times 2 = 2 \times 5 = 10\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}$ in both states ✓.
